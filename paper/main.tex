\begin{abstract}
A fallback trigger decides when an automated vehicle should abandon
its task for a minimum-risk manoeuvre (MRM). In practice it is a
hand-tuned threshold with no stated guarantee, and statistical
certification of such thresholds has been assessed on predictions or
static labels rather than on what the fallback then does. We ask how
reliable a certified trigger is when judged through actual interventions,
and what happens to it under dataset shift. A rollout depends only on the
tick at which the fallback fires, so one reference run and one
forced-firing rollout per decision tick evaluate every trigger,
threshold, online method and latency exactly, on identical physics. On
replayed Argoverse~2 and nuScenes logs with reactive traffic, a
UN~R157 manoeuvre and nuPlan harm labels, we compare a
Learn-then-Test-certified geometric trigger with seven baselines. At a
5\,\% target, the certified trigger misses 3.7\,\% of harmful events and
exceeds the target in 9.5\,\% of 200 resplits (limit 10\,\%); the same
score tuned without a certificate exceeds it in 43.5\,\%. That validity
costs 2.5 points of unnecessary stops (28.5\,\% versus 26.0\,\%), so we
do not claim a better trade-off. Confidence and ensemble triggers need
68--71\,\% unnecessary stops for comparable safety. A threshold certified
on Argoverse~2 misses 13.2\,\% on nuScenes, 5.0\,\% of nuScenes scenarios
contain harm no trigger can prevent, and one decision tick (0.5\,s) of
latency already breaks the target. In an exploratory analysis, a few
dozen labelled target scenes restore a valid certificate but at a large
cost in stops. [Waymo replication to be inserted.]
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} vehicles that lack confidence in their own
situational understanding are expected to fall back on a minimum-risk
manoeuvre: slow to a stop, pull to the shoulder, or otherwise hand
control back to a human. The decision of \emph{when} to invoke this
behaviour is, in nearly every deployed and published system, a
hand-tuned threshold on some scalar confidence or uncertainty score
produced by a perception or prediction module. That practice has a
specific and largely unexamined weakness: the very quantity the
threshold is applied to — a learned model's uncertainty estimate — is
exactly what has been shown to degrade under the kind of distribution
shift a vehicle experiences when it enters a new city, a new weather
condition, or simply a stretch of road unlike anything in its
calibration set. A threshold tuned under one operating distribution
carries no guarantee once that distribution has moved, and the failure
is silent: the system continues to report a confidence number, and nothing
in the number itself signals that the number is now wrong.

Conformal prediction offers a principled alternative: rather than a
point confidence score, it produces a prediction region with a
distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantee under
exchangeability. A substantial and fast-growing literature has applied
conformal prediction to trajectory forecasting and to planning under
uncertainty in autonomous driving. Almost without exception, however,
this literature is \emph{open-loop}: it constructs a prediction region,
reports its coverage and its size, and stops. Two things are invisible
from that vantage point. First, a region's effect on a downstream
control decision — specifically, whether it is used to trigger a
fallback — is not evaluated at all; open-loop coverage and closed-loop
safety outcome are simply assumed to move together. Second, and more
consequentially, \emph{triggering the fallback is not free}. A vehicle
that stops in a live lane because its uncertainty region grew large is
not obviously safer than one that continues; an evaluation that reports
only coverage, or only collision rate under the fallback, cannot see the
trade-off between the two, and cannot see that a policy which triggers
whenever it is asked to would trivially achieve a good miss rate while
being useless.

This paper asks a narrower question than ``which trigger is best'':
\emph{how reliable is a statistically certified fallback trigger when it
is judged through the interventions it actually causes, and what happens
to it when the deployment data differ from the calibration data?} We
treat the trigger as a decision problem with a formal guarantee attached
to the decision itself, and evaluate it where that guarantee has to hold:
in closed loop, on real driving scenarios, with a threshold whose
consequences change what the vehicle does next. Certifying a threshold on
a risk score against missed events is not new; Luo~\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2022}
do so with conformal prediction, and Conformal Decision
Theory~\cite{cdt2024} calibrates decisions rather than predictions
through a long-run \emph{average} guarantee. We build on these and do not
claim the certification step as ours. An MRM trigger nevertheless differs
in kind from the settings they study. It is an irreversible, one-shot
decision to abandon the task, its errors are paid in two currencies
(missed interventions, and the harm of stopping itself), and a deployed
vehicle needs its guarantee before the first mile.

We organise the study around four research questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Does a certificate keep its promise when measured on
the closed-loop outcome, and what does keeping it cost in unnecessary
stops and fallback-induced collisions?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Do open-loop failure-detection metrics choose the
same trigger that closed-loop safety does?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} What happens to a certificate under a real
cross-dataset shift, and how much can be repaired with unlabelled or
with a few labelled target scenarios?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} How sensitive is the certificate to the timing of
the intervention?
\end{itemize}

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An exact closed-loop evaluation protocol.} A rollout's
outcome depends only on \emph{when} the fallback fires. One reference run
that records every trigger score, plus one rollout forced to fire at each
decision tick, evaluates every trigger, threshold, online method and
latency on identical physics, with the residual simulator drift measured
and reported (Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}).
\item \textbf{What certification buys and what it costs, in closed loop.}
We price each operating point in missed interventions, unnecessary stops
and fallback-induced collisions. Certification is the only design that
reliably keeps its target across resplits, at a measured cost in stops.
It does not win the safety--conservatism trade-off against a tuned
threshold, a hypothesis we pre-registered and report as refuted.
Confidence and ensemble triggers, the deployed practice, need several
times more unnecessary stops than a geometric score for comparable
safety.
\item \textbf{The certificate under real dataset shift.} An Argoverse~2
certificate fails on nuScenes. The nuScenes data also expose a floor:
harm present at the first decision tick, which no trigger can prevent and
which can make a target unreachable. A density-ratio-weighted certificate
that reports its effective sample size repairs part of the gap, and in an
exploratory analysis the transfer is asymmetric.
\item \textbf{Operating-point findings for practitioners.} Latency of one
decision tick breaks the target, route-following ego corridors make a
trigger fire in every scenario, and open-loop AUROC ranks triggers only
approximately as closed-loop stops do.
\end{enumerate}

All hypotheses, thresholds and refutation rules were recorded in a
version-controlled note before the data were collected. Refuted and
unsupported hypotheses are reported as such, and analyses run after the
pre-registered verdicts are labelled exploratory.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} situates this work against open-loop
conformal prediction for driving, task-relevant failure detection, and
closed-loop safety evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:method}
formalises the triggering problem and the certification procedure.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the closed-loop evaluation
environment, the fallback manoeuvre, the harm labels and the baselines.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results, ablations and the shift
experiments. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} states the scope of the
claims, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Conformal prediction for autonomous driving}
Conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005} constructs prediction sets with a
distribution-free, finite-sample marginal coverage guarantee under
exchangeability between calibration and test data, and has been applied
extensively to trajectory forecasting and to safe planning under
uncertainty. Lindemann~\emph{et al.} embed conformal prediction regions
directly into a model predictive controller to obtain probabilistic
safety guarantees, but their guarantee collapses once the deployment
distribution shifts away from the calibration distribution — a
limitation Rahaman~\emph{et al.}~\cite{robustcp2026} address with robust
conformal prediction driven by a conditional generative model of the
shift, evaluated in a synthetic multi-agent corridor. SafePath
\cite{safepath2025} integrates conformal prediction into an LLM-based
path-planning pipeline, providing a guarantee that a set of candidate
paths contains a safe one with a user-specified probability, and reports
both open-loop (nuScenes) and closed-loop (highway-env, synthetic
traffic) results. In all three cases the guarantee is on the
\emph{prediction} — a region contains the truth, or a path set contains
a safe path — rather than on the eventual control decision the region is
used to make, and none evaluates a fallback-triggering decision on
real, replayed driving logs under reactive traffic. This paper's
guarantee is instead on the decision: the probability that a
minimum-risk manoeuvre fires before a harmful event, evaluated
end-to-end in closed loop.

\subsection{Online and decision-level conformal methods}
Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI)~\cite{aci2021} updates the
miscoverage level online so that long-run coverage holds even under
arbitrary shift. Conformal Decision Theory (CDT)~\cite{cdt2024} carries
the same idea from prediction sets to decisions: it updates a
decision parameter $\lambda_{t+1} = \lambda_t + \eta(\varepsilon - \ell_t)$
so that the long-run average risk converges to $\varepsilon$ at rate
$O(1/t)$, with no distributional assumptions. It is demonstrated on
robot navigation among pedestrians (Stanford Drone Dataset), with a
continuous distance-based loss. Both give \emph{asymptotic, average}
guarantees for a quantity adjusted continuously as data arrive. We
target a different object: a one-shot, irreversible trigger whose
certificate must hold with finite samples before deployment, and whose
errors include the harm that triggering itself causes. We adopt both
methods as baselines (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), applied
episode-by-episode, which is the natural online counterpart of our
setting.

\subsection{Task-relevant and closed-loop failure detection}
Farid~\emph{et al.}~\cite{taskrelevant2022} propose a run-time monitor
for trajectory-prediction failures that is \emph{task-relevant} by
construction: it flags a misprediction only when the resulting planning
cost is anomalous relative to the predicted cost distribution, with
data-free false-positive and false-negative rate bounds. Their detector
is evaluated against hand-labelled scenarios in an open-loop and
semi-reactive setting, and — like the conformal-prediction literature
above — does not close the loop through an actuated fallback and
re-measure the outcome. Their central insight, that a
task-irrelevant misprediction should not count against a detector, is
reflected in this paper's harm labelling: a collision the ego vehicle
suffers while already stopped or while being struck from behind is
scored as a distinct \emph{induced-collision} cost of the fallback
itself, never as a missed intervention.

Closest to our certification step, Luo~\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2022}
calibrate the alert threshold of a warning system with conformal
prediction so that the fraction of unsafe situations that raise no alert
is bounded with finite samples, and demonstrate it on driving. We
adopt the same principle — certify a threshold on a risk score against
missed events — and do not claim it as new. What we add is what happens
\emph{after} the alert: the fallback it triggers changes the outcome
(it can prevent harm, cause an unnecessary stop, or induce a rear-end
collision), so miss and over-intervention rates can only be measured by
closing the loop. Our contributions are an exact way to do that for
every trigger, threshold and online method on identical physics, the
resulting test of whether open-loop failure-detection metrics select the
right trigger, and the behaviour of the certificate under a real
cross-dataset shift.

\subsection{Risk-sensitive safety analysis}
Chapman~\emph{et al.}~\cite{cvar2022} develop risk-sensitive safe sets
using Conditional Value-at-Risk to assess the severity, not merely the
probability, of constraint violation along a stochastic trajectory,
providing the risk-sensitive vocabulary this paper borrows in
motivating why a mean missed-intervention rate is the appropriate
quantity to control at a chosen operating point, even though a
tail-severity extension (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) is left to future
work.

\subsection{Closed-loop simulation infrastructure}
ScenarioNet~\cite{scenarionet2023} unifies heterogeneous real-world
driving logs — including Argoverse~2, nuScenes, nuPlan, and Lyft
Level~5 — into a common scenario description that can be replayed
interactively in the MetaDrive simulator, with either logged-trajectory
replay or reactive rule-based (IDM) traffic. This work is built directly
on that infrastructure: every scenario reported in
Section~\ref{sec:results} is a real logged driving scenario, replayed
headless with reactive traffic, not a synthetic or hand-crafted one.

\section{Problem Formulation and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Fallback triggering as selective prediction}
\label{sec:formulation}
Consider a closed-loop rollout of a scenario $X$ under a fallback
policy governed by a scalar threshold $\lambda$: at every decision tick
the vehicle computes an uncertainty score $u_t(X)$ from a trajectory
predictor's output for nearby agents, and engages the minimum-risk
manoeuvre the first time $u_t(X) > \lambda$, remaining engaged for the
rest of the episode. Define the closed-loop loss
\begin{equation}
L(X,\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\, \text{harm in } X \text{ under }
\lambda,\ \text{MRM not first} \,\bigr],
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
the \emph{missed-intervention} indicator, and the complementary
over-conservatism quantity
\begin{equation}
S(X,\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\, \text{MRM fires under } \lambda,\
X \text{ harm-free at } \lambda=\infty \,\bigr],
\label{eq:stop}
\end{equation}
the \emph{unnecessary stop} indicator, evaluated against the
counterfactual rollout of the same scenario with the trigger disabled.
The goal is to choose $\hat\lambda$ such that
\begin{equation}
\Pr_{X_1,\dots,X_n}\Bigl(\, \mathbb{E}_X\bigl[L(X,\hat\lambda)\bigr] \le
\alpha \,\Bigr) \ge 1-\delta,
\label{eq:guarantee}
\end{equation}
while minimising $\mathbb{E}_X[S(X,\hat\lambda)]$ — the smallest
autonomy loss compatible with the risk target $\alpha$.

Equation~\eqref{eq:guarantee} is not a conformal coverage statement:
$L(X,\lambda)$ depends on the entire closed-loop rollout under
$\lambda$, since engaging the fallback changes every subsequent state,
so it is neither a marginal coverage event on a static prediction nor
guaranteed to be monotone in $\lambda$ a priori. We therefore calibrate
$\hat\lambda$ with Learn-then-Test~\cite{ltt2021}: for a
finite grid $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 > \cdots > \lambda_J$ ordered from
least to most conservative, compute the empirical risk
$\hat R(\lambda_j) = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i L(X_i,\lambda_j)$ on $n$
calibration scenarios, a Hoeffding--Bentkus $p$-value for the null
hypothesis $H_j: \mathbb{E}[L(\cdot,\lambda_j)] > \alpha$, and apply
fixed-sequence testing starting from the most conservative $\lambda$;
every $\lambda_j$ whose null is rejected satisfies
\eqref{eq:guarantee} simultaneously, with family-wise error controlled
at $\delta$, and $\hat\lambda$ is the least conservative such $\lambda_j$
(largest autonomy). Where the empirical loss is observed to be monotone
in $\lambda$ across the calibration set, we additionally report
Conformal Risk Control~\cite{crc2022}, whose expectation guarantee is
tighter but requires that monotonicity assumption; where it is violated
we report the violation rate directly rather than silently falling back
to the weaker method.

\subsection{Trigger score}
\label{sec:trigger-score}
At each decision tick the trigger score $u_t$ measures how far a
predicted future position of any nearby agent intrudes into the ego
vehicle's own near-term extent. Writing the ego's constant-heading,
constant-speed extrapolation over a horizon of $T$ steps as
$\{p^{\mathrm{ego}}_\tau\}_{\tau=1}^{T}$ — the same route-agnostic
motion model used by the harm metric in
Section~\ref{sec:harm}, deliberately \emph{not} the vehicle's planned,
route-following path, so that the trigger cannot credit itself for
steering behaviour the safety label does not assume — and a
predictor's $K$-mode forecast for agent $i$ as
$\{\hat p^{(i,k)}_\tau\}$, each predicted offset is decomposed into a
longitudinal component $a^{(i,k)}_\tau$ (along the ego heading) and a
lateral component $\ell^{(i,k)}_\tau$ (perpendicular to it), each
compared against a separate margin: a length-based longitudinal margin
$m^{\mathrm{lon}}_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(L_{\mathrm{ego}}+L_i)+c_{\mathrm{lon}}$
and a width-based lateral margin
$m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(W_{\mathrm{ego}}+W_i)+c_{\mathrm{lat}}$.
The intrusion at $(i,k,\tau)$ is
\begin{equation}
\rho^{(i,k)}_\tau = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \sqrt{
\left(\frac{a^{(i,k)}_\tau}{m^{\mathrm{lon}}_i}\right)^{\!2} +
\left(\frac{\ell^{(i,k)}_\tau}{m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i}\right)^{\!2}}\;
\right) m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
and $u_t = \max_{i,k,\tau} \rho^{(i,k)}_\tau$. A single isotropic
clearance radius cannot represent both margins at once: a radius set by
width alone risks missing a straight-ahead lead vehicle, and one set by
the half-diagonal risks triggering on adjacent-lane traffic. The
decomposition in \eqref{eq:score} is therefore a modelling choice made
during development on this reasoning. Our ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) finds that it gives no measurable benefit
over a width-only isotropic radius, so we report it as a design
option rather than a requirement.

\subsection{Fallback manoeuvre}
\label{sec:mrm}
On triggering, the vehicle executes the minimum-risk manoeuvre defined
by UN Regulation No.~157 (Automated Lane Keeping Systems)
\S\,5.5.1~\cite{unr157}: decelerate inside the current lane with a
deceleration demand not exceeding $4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, lane-keeping
control otherwise unchanged, holding the vehicle stationary once
stopped. This is enforced by a proportional controller tracking the
target speed profile $v(t) = \max(v_0 - 4.0\,t,\,0)$ from the moment of
triggering.

\subsection{Certification under covariate shift}
\label{sec:shift}
The certificate in \eqref{eq:guarantee} assumes calibration and
deployment scenarios are exchangeable. When the deployment distribution
differs by covariate shift, we reweight calibration scenarios by an
estimated density ratio $w(x) \propto p_{\mathrm{target}}(x) /
p_{\mathrm{source}}(x)$ computed from pre-deployment scenario covariates
only (agent counts and speeds, initial gap, route length). A
probabilistic domain classifier is trained on unlabelled target
scenarios, following weighted conformal prediction~\cite{tibshirani2019}.
The weighted empirical risk $\hat R_w(\lambda) = \sum_i w_i L(X_i,\lambda)
/ \sum_i w_i$ is tested with the Hoeffding--Bentkus $p$-value at the
Kish effective sample size $n_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i
w_i^2$. As the weights concentrate, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ shrinks and the
test certifies a more conservative threshold, and eventually none at
all. The procedure thus reports when the source data cannot support a
guarantee for the target, rather than asserting one that has silently
expired.

\subsection{Exact counterfactual evaluation}
\label{sec:exact-eval}
Two rollouts of the same scenario are identical until one of them
triggers the fallback, up to the simulator's own non-determinism. We
measure this in every forced-firing rollout. Over the first 33{,}761
forced-firing rollouts (2{,}000 scenarios), 98.4\,\% agreed with the
reference run to within 1\,cm before firing. The pre-fire time-to-collision
trace differed in 1.2\,\% and the pre-fire harm status in one rollout
(0.003\,\%). We report the result of every analysis both on all scenarios
and after dropping the 3.6\,\% of scenarios with any flagged drift. A rollout's outcome is
therefore a function of the \emph{firing tick} alone, not of which score,
threshold or update rule caused the firing. For each scenario we
simulate one reference run with the fallback disabled, recording every
trigger score at every decision tick, and one rollout forced to fire at
each decision tick. Any rule of the form ``fire at the first tick where
$u_t > \lambda$'' is then an exact lookup. This includes online rules,
whose $\lambda$ changes between scenarios, and added actuation latency.
All methods in Section~\ref{sec:results} are compared on the same
simulated physics, so differences between them cannot come from
simulator noise or from unequal compute budgets.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Simulation environment}
All rollouts are executed headless in MetaDrive, replaying scenarios
converted by ScenarioNet~\cite{scenarionet2023} from Argoverse~2 Motion
Forecasting and nuScenes. Non-ego traffic is controlled reactively
(rule-based, IDM), not replayed open-loop from the log: pure log
replay was found to roughly triple the no-fallback collision rate on a
1{,}000-scenario diagnostic sample, because logged agents cannot react
to an ego vehicle that deviates from its logged trajectory — precisely
the deviation a fallback manoeuvre produces. Reporting results under
non-reactive replay would therefore misrepresent the cost of stopping,
which is a central quantity this paper reports.

\subsection{Harm labelling}
\label{sec:harm}
A rollout is labelled harmful using the nuPlan closed-loop safety
definition~\cite{nuplan2024}: an at-fault collision, or a
time-to-collision below $0.95\,\mathrm{s}$ computed over a $3.0\,$s
horizon at $0.1\,$s resolution, not evaluated while the ego is
stationary and not evaluated against tracks behind the ego. Fault
attribution follows the nuPlan convention: a collision the ego suffers
while stationary, or while being struck from behind, is \emph{not}
at-fault and is never counted as a missed intervention; it is instead
recorded as an \emph{induced collision} — the concrete, measurable cost
of a false or premature trigger.

\subsection{Predictor}
The trajectory predictor is AutoBot~\cite{autobot2022} (6 modes, 2.1\,s
history, 6\,s horizon), trained with the UniTraj framework~\cite{unitraj2024}
on the Argoverse~2 Motion Forecasting training split (minADE$_6$ = 1.09\,m
on held-out AV2 validation scenes). It runs online inside the closed loop, so its input is the actual,
possibly ego-deviated, simulator history rather than the original log.
This is necessary once traffic is reactive. The model's own UniTraj
preprocessing is reused unchanged, so it sees its training input
distribution. Vehicles are predicted by the network. Pedestrians and
cyclists, which the checkpoint was not trained on, receive
constant-velocity forecasts.

\subsection{Datasets and splits}
\label{sec:data}
All scenarios come from Argoverse~2 validation, disjoint from the
predictor's training data. They are split deterministically by scenario
identifier into development (14{,}990), calibration (4{,}971) and test
(5{,}027) sets. The shifted target is nuScenes (9{,}041 scenarios of
8.1\,s, Boston and Singapore, versus Argoverse~2's six U.S. cities).
Scenarios in which the ego moves less than 10\,m are excluded, as in
ScenarioNet. Design choices were made on the development split only.
The pre-registered evaluation uses 2{,}000 calibration, 2{,}000 test
and 1{,}500 nuScenes scenarios. Ten Argoverse~2 scenarios (six
calibration, four test) whose per-agent records could not be matched
after a re-scoring pass are dropped from every analysis, so all methods
are compared on identical scenarios; this leaves 1{,}994 calibration and
1{,}996 test scenarios, and 1{,}504 nuScenes scenarios. A second shifted
target, Waymo Open Motion~\cite{waymo2021} validation scenarios (9.1\,s,
1\,s of history), is collected under a separate pre-registered amendment
[results pending]. Decisions are taken at 2\,Hz. A paired
development-split comparison showed identical miss rates at 2\,Hz and
10\,Hz, and a 10\,Hz replication on 100 test scenarios is reported.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
All methods use the same predictor, the same MRM and the same test
scenarios, and are evaluated exactly (Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{T1 --- confidence threshold} (deployed practice): fire when
$1-\max_k p_k$ of the network-predicted vehicle whose predicted modes come
closest to the ego exceeds a threshold. Agents the network does not
predict (pedestrians, cyclists, distant vehicles) are excluded from this
score. The threshold is tuned on calibration data to the most permissive
value with empirical miss rate $\le\alpha$. A variant that uses the
nearest agent ahead is also evaluated.
\item \textbf{T2 --- ensemble threshold}: the same tuning, applied to the
spread of endpoint predictions across five Monte Carlo dropout passes
\cite{galghahramani2016}.
\item \textbf{T3 --- open-loop conformal}: inflate every predicted mode by
the split-conformal radius of the predictor's 3\,s error on held-out
Argoverse~2 logs at level $\alpha$~\cite{vovk2005}, and fire when the
inflated region touches the ego. This is the guarantee-on-the-prediction
design that the paper argues is insufficient.
\item \textbf{Tuned geometric}: our score with an empirically tuned
threshold and no certificate. It isolates the contribution of the
calibrator.
\item \textbf{CDT}~\cite{cdt2024} and \textbf{ACI}~\cite{aci2021}: online
decision- and prediction-level adaptation, updated after every
scenario. Each uses the step size that performs best on test, which
favours the baselines.
\item \textbf{Oracle}: the best threshold in hindsight on test data.
It is not deployable and is reported as an upper bound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
For every method we report the missed-intervention, unnecessary-stop and
fallback-induced-collision rates, autonomy (the fraction of scenarios
completed without the fallback) and route completion, with Wilson 95\,\%
intervals. Validity of each certificate is assessed over 200 random
resplits of the pooled calibration and test scenarios. A valid
certificate keeps the frequency of test miss rate above $\alpha$ at or
below $\delta$. Harm is scored with the single nuPlan definition above; its sensitivity
to the time-to-collision threshold is not evaluated here
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Pre-registration and reproducibility}
The harm definition, $\alpha = 0.05$, $\delta = 0.10$, and the refutation
criteria for three hypotheses were recorded in a version-controlled note
before any calibration or test data were collected: \textbf{H1}, that
open-loop metrics rank triggers differently from closed-loop outcomes;
\textbf{H2}, that a certified trigger achieves a better
safety--conservatism trade-off than a tuned fixed threshold; and
\textbf{H3}, that a threshold certified on Argoverse~2 loses its
guarantee on nuScenes. The note carries dated amendments (a corrected
confidence baseline, the exactness measurements, a Waymo target and a
reverse-shift analysis), each written before the corresponding
outcomes were evaluated, and it labels the analyses run afterwards as
post hoc. Code, per-scenario rollout records and configuration files
will be released with the paper. Each scenario's record contains every
score trace and outcome, so every table can be regenerated without

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All results use $\alpha = 0.05$ and $\delta = 0.10$, 1{,}994 calibration
and 1{,}996 test scenarios from Argoverse~2, and 1{,}504 nuScenes
scenarios, with Wilson 95\,\% intervals unless stated. Dropping the
3.6\,\% of scenarios with any flagged simulator drift changes no
conclusion below. Sections~\ref{sec:res-main}--\ref{sec:res-shift} are
the pre-registered analyses. Sections~\ref{sec:res-reverse}
and~\ref{sec:res-fewshot} are post hoc and labelled as such.

\subsection{Does a certificate keep its promise, and what does it cost? (RQ1)}
\label{sec:res-main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives every method at its operating point on the
Argoverse~2 test set. The certified trigger (LTT) misses 3.7\,\% of
harmful events [95\,\% CI 2.9--4.6], below the 5\,\% target, with
28.5\,\% unnecessary stops and 2.6\,\% fallback-induced collisions.
The last column repeats the calibration on 200 random resplits and
reports how often the realised test miss rate exceeds $\alpha$. A valid
certificate keeps this at or below $\delta = 10\,\%$. LTT does (9.5\,\%).
The same geometric score with a threshold tuned empirically, and its
Conformal Risk Control (CRC) counterpart, which guarantees only an
expectation, exceed the target in 43.5\,\% and 41.0\,\% of resplits, and
the tuned confidence trigger in 50.5\,\%. Certification is therefore the
only design here that keeps its target reliably.

\emph{The certificate has a price, and it does not win the trade-off.}
The tuned geometric threshold stops less often than LTT (26.0\,\% versus
28.5\,\%; paired bootstrap difference $-2.5$ points, 95\,\% CI
$[-3.2,-1.8]$), and CDT stops less still (24.1\,\%). Our pre-registered
hypothesis H2 required no baseline to be within 3 points of the
certified trigger; it is \textbf{refuted}. The supportable claim is
narrower: among the designs whose promise can be checked, the certified
one is the only one that reliably meets it, and it pays about 2.5 points
of unnecessary stops for that.

\emph{Deployed-practice triggers are far more conservative.} Tuned to
the same miss target, the confidence and MC-dropout triggers stop in 68.3\,\% and
70.8\,\% of scenarios, 40--45 points more than the certified geometric
trigger (paired CIs exclude zero by a wide margin), and the open-loop
conformal trigger reaches a miss rate of 0.0\,\% only by firing in
73.8\,\%. Its certificate is valid in the sense that it never violates,
and useless in the sense that a vehicle that stops in three of four
scenarios has abandoned its task. ACI reaches 1.3\,\% misses at 45\,\%
stops, which is consistent with a guarantee on the prediction not
translating into a useful decision.

\subsection{Do open-loop metrics choose the closed-loop trigger? (RQ2)}
\label{sec:res-h1}
Table~\ref{tab:h1} ranks six trigger scores by open-loop AUROC of their
scenario peak for predicting harm, and by closed-loop unnecessary stops
at each score's certified threshold. Rankings agree at the top and
diverge below it: Kendall's $\tau = 0.60$ on Argoverse~2 and $0.47$ on
nuScenes, and the top-ranked score is the same on Argoverse~2 and differs
on nuScenes only by 0.2 points of stops. The pre-registered rule
refutes H1 only if $\tau \ge 0.8$ and the top score coincides in every
setting, so H1 is not refuted, but the evidence is weak: open-loop AUROC
picked the closed-loop best score, or a statistical tie, in every
setting. The clearest disagreement is at the bottom. The confidence score
has AUROC $0.45$, below chance, meaning higher confidence-based
uncertainty is on average associated with \emph{less} harm, yet a
threshold on it can still reach the miss target, at 75.8\,\% stops
(certified) rather than by discriminating harmful from safe scenarios.

Table~\ref{tab:abl} shows that two components matter and one does not.
Using the vehicle's planned, route-following path instead of a kinematic
corridor makes the trigger fire in essentially every scenario (84.9\,\%

stops fall, so a certificate calibrated at zero latency is not valid for
a slower actuation chain and latency belongs in the certified quantity.

The Argoverse~2 certificate does not transfer. On nuScenes the threshold
certified at a 5\,\% target misses 13.2\,\% [11.6, 15.0], 2.6 times the
target and with an interval that excludes it; the pre-registered
hypothesis H3 is \textbf{supported} (15.2\,\% after dropping drift
scenarios). Part of this is a floor rather than shift: 5.0\,\% of
nuScenes scenarios already contain harm at the first decision tick, which
even a trigger that always fires cannot prevent (0.1\,\% on
Argoverse~2). That floor equals the target, so no threshold can be
certified at $\alpha = 0.05$ on nuScenes. With those scenarios excluded
(post hoc), the shifted miss rate is 9.2\,\% [7.8, 10.8], still above the
target.

Density-ratio weighting, using only unlabelled target covariates, moves
the miss rate from 13.2\,\% to 7.0\,\%, still above the target, and
doubles unnecessary stops (26.1\,\% to 52.4\,\%). On the avoidable subset
it reaches 2.2\,\%, below the target, but its effective sample size is only
68, so the certificate is achieved almost entirely through conservatism
(54.5\,\% stops).

\subsection{Reverse shift (exploratory, post hoc)}
\label{sec:res-reverse}
We also certified on half of the nuScenes scenarios and deployed on
Argoverse~2 test, a direction pre-registered as a reverse-shift analysis.
As pre-registered, with all scenarios, the procedure \emph{refused to
certify} on both halves, because of the 5.0\,\% floor. On the avoidable
subset (post hoc), thresholds certified on nuScenes ($n\!\approx\!715$)
give an Argoverse~2 miss rate of 1.9\,\% and 2.0\,\%, both below the
target, at 40.6\,\% and 39.5\,\% unnecessary stops. A certificate from the
harder dataset therefore transfers safely but conservatively, while the
reverse does not transfer. This asymmetry is a single-pair observation
pending replication.

\subsection{Recalibration with a few labelled target scenes (exploratory)}
\label{sec:res-fewshot}

\section{Scope and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}
The claims above are deliberately scoped. Each boundary below is a
design choice with a stated rationale, and each suggests a direct
extension.

\emph{What we claim, and what we do not.} We claim that certification is
what makes a closed-loop trigger keep its stated miss rate, that it costs
a measurable number of unnecessary stops, and that the guarantee is
fragile to dataset shift and to latency. We do not claim a
better-performing trigger: a tuned threshold and an online method stop
less often, and we report the pre-registered hypothesis that said
otherwise as refuted.

\emph{One predictor, one simulator.} All results use one trajectory
predictor trained on Argoverse~2, and MetaDrive with rule-based (IDM)
agents. Whether the ordering of triggers survives a stronger predictor
or learned agent behaviour is open. The protocol accepts both unchanged,
and the geometric-versus-confidence gap is large enough that we expect it
to persist, which we have not tested.

\emph{Unavoidable harm.} The 5.0\,\% first-tick floor on nuScenes shows
that a miss metric defined on harm anywhere in a replayed snippet mixes
events a fallback could prevent with events already under way. We report
the pre-registered definition and the avoidable-only variant side by side,
and propose the latter as a refinement for future benchmarks.

\emph{Marginal, scenario-level guarantees.} The certificate bounds the
miss rate over the scenario distribution, the quantity a deployment
authority can audit. It does not bound the risk of any individual
scenario. Stratified certification by scenario type follows from the
same procedure applied within strata.

\emph{Covariate shift and few-shot recalibration.} The weighted certificate
covers shift in observable scenario covariates, and its effective sample
size, 68 in our nuScenes subset, shows when it becomes unreliable. The
few-label recalibration result is exploratory and single-pair; it needs
replication on a second target before being relied on.

\emph{Harm definition.} Harm is scored with the nuPlan definition alone.
Sensitivity to the time-to-collision threshold and to fault attribution is
a natural robustness study, and a severity-weighted or tail (CVaR)
certificate in the sense of~\cite{cvar2022} requires only a change of loss.

\emph{Object states as input; one fallback family.} The predictor receives
agent states, isolating the triggering decision from perception error, and
we evaluate stop-in-lane manoeuvres at the UN~R157 deceleration limit.
Composing the certificate with a calibrated perception module, and other
fallbacks such as a lateral pull-over or hand-over, change the quantities
priced but not the method.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
A safety guarantee on a fallback trigger is not a permanent property of the
model; it depends on the data it was certified on, the timing of the
intervention, and the consequences of the fallback itself. Evaluated
exactly in closed loop, a Learn-then-Test-certified trigger keeps its
5\,\% target on the calibration distribution (3.7\,\% missed, violations in
9.5\,\% of resplits against 43.5\,\% without a certificate) at a cost of
about 2.5 points of unnecessary stops, and deployed-practice confidence and
ensemble triggers need 68--71\,\% stops for similar safety. The same
certificate fails on nuScenes (13.2\,\% missed), is broken by one decision
tick of latency, and can be unreachable when the target data contain harm
no trigger can prevent. Recalibrating with a few dozen labelled target
scenes restores a valid certificate at a large operating cost in an
exploratory analysis. [Waymo replication to be added.] These findings
argue for treating certification, shift monitoring and latency as one
audited quantity, and the exact evaluation protocol makes that audit
inexpensive to repeat for any trigger.
