\begin{abstract}
A fallback trigger decides when an automated vehicle should abandon
its task for a minimum-risk maneuver. In practice it is a hand-tuned
threshold with no stated guarantee, and statistical certification of
alert thresholds rarely prices what the fallback then does. We ask how
reliable a certified trigger is when the fallback is executed, and what
happens under dataset shift. Because a rollout depends only on the tick
at which the fallback fires, one reference run plus one forced rollout
per decision tick evaluates every trigger, threshold, online method and
latency by lookup, which we check against direct execution. On replayed Argoverse~2, nuScenes and Waymo logs with
reactive traffic, a regulation-compliant in-lane stop and nuPlan harm
labels, we compare a Learn-then-Test-certified geometric trigger with
seven baselines. At a 5\,\% target, the certified trigger misses
3.7\,\% of scenarios and exceeds the target in 9.5\,\% of 200 resplits,
against 43.5\,\% for the same score tuned without a certificate, which
stops less often. Certification buys reliability, not fewer stops. It
also controls the alert, not the outcome: harm in the intervened run is
6.5\,\%, and 24\,\% of harmful scenarios go unalerted. Confidence and
ensemble triggers need 68--71\,\% unnecessary stops. The certificate
fails on nuScenes (13.2\,\% missed) but holds on Waymo (2.7\,\%), so
failure under shift is dataset-dependent. One decision tick (0.5\,s)
of latency already breaks the target.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} vehicles that lack confidence in their own
situational understanding are expected to fall back on a minimum-risk
maneuver (MRM): slow to a stop, pull to the shoulder, or hand control
back to a human. When to invoke it is, in most deployed and published
systems, a hand-tuned threshold on a scalar confidence or uncertainty
score from a perception or prediction module. Such a threshold carries
no guarantee, and learned uncertainty estimates are known to degrade
under the shift a vehicle meets in a new city or weather. The failure
is silent: the system keeps reporting a confidence number that is no
longer right.

Conformal prediction offers a principled alternative, a
distribution-free, finite-sample guarantee under exchangeability, and
has been applied widely to trajectory forecasting and planning in
driving. A guarantee on a prediction, or on a warning, is nevertheless
not a guarantee on the consequences of the action it triggers. Whether
an alert leads to a fallback is rarely evaluated in closed loop, and
triggering is not free: a vehicle that stops in a live lane because its
uncertainty grew is not obviously safer than one that continues, and a
trigger that fires whenever asked trivially achieves a good miss rate
while being useless. Reporting only coverage, or only collision rate,
hides this trade-off.

We ask a narrower question than ``which trigger is best'':
\emph{how reliable is a statistically certified fallback trigger when it
is judged through the interventions it actually causes, and what happens
to it when the deployment data differ from the calibration data?} We
treat the trigger as a decision problem with a formal guarantee attached
to the decision itself, and evaluate it where that guarantee has to hold:
in closed loop, on real driving scenarios, with a threshold whose
consequences change what the vehicle does next. Certifying a threshold on
a risk score against missed events is not new. Luo~\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2022}
do so with conformal prediction, and Conformal Decision
Theory~\cite{cdt2024} calibrates decisions rather than predictions
through a long-run \emph{average} guarantee. We build on these and do not
claim the certification step as ours. An MRM trigger nevertheless differs
in kind from the settings they study. It is an irreversible, one-shot
decision to abandon the task, its errors are paid in two currencies
(missed interventions, and the harm of stopping itself), and a deployed
vehicle needs its guarantee before the first mile. To our knowledge, no
prior work certifies a fallback trigger and then measures, in closed
loop, what the executed fallback does.

We organize the study around four research questions, each tied to a
hypothesis fixed before data collection (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (H2).} Does a certificate keep its promise when
measured on the closed-loop outcome, and what does keeping it cost in
unnecessary stops and fallback-induced collisions?
\item \textbf{RQ2 (H1).} Do open-loop failure-detection metrics choose
the same trigger that closed-loop safety does?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (H3, H3-W).} What happens to a certificate under a
real cross-dataset shift, and how much can be repaired with unlabeled or
with a few labeled target scenarios?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} How sensitive is the certificate to the timing of
the intervention?
\end{itemize}

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A lookup evaluation protocol for executed fallbacks.}
Under a determinism assumption whose violation we measure, a rollout's
outcome depends only on \emph{when} the fallback fires
(Observation~\ref{prop:exact}, Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}). One reference run that records every
trigger score, plus one rollout forced to fire at each decision tick
(about 17 per scenario), evaluates every trigger, threshold, online
method and latency on identical physics, where direct execution would
need over 7{,}000 rollouts per scenario here. We check the lookup against
direct execution (Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}). The forced rollouts also
expose the consequences of the fallback itself: induced collisions, route
completion, and harm that persists or arises after it fires.
\item \textbf{What certification buys and what it costs.} Certification
is the only design that reliably keeps its target across resplits, at a
measured cost in stops. It does not win the safety--conservatism
trade-off against a tuned threshold, a hypothesis we pre-registered and
report as refuted. The certified quantity is the alert, not the outcome:
residual harm after the fallback is larger than the certified miss rate,
part of it created by the intervention, and certifying residual harm
directly was not achievable at a useful operating point. Confidence and
ensemble triggers,
the deployed practice, need several times more unnecessary stops than a
geometric score.
\item \textbf{The certificate under real dataset shift.} An Argoverse~2
certificate fails on nuScenes but holds, conservatively, on Waymo: the
failure is dataset-dependent, not universal. The nuScenes data also
expose a floor, harm present at the first decision tick, which no
trigger can prevent and which can make a target unreachable. A
density-ratio-weighted certificate that reports its effective sample
size repairs part of the gap.
\item \textbf{Operating-point findings for practitioners.} One decision
tick of actuation latency breaks the target, so latency must be part of
the certified quantity, and open-loop AUROC ranks triggers only
approximately as closed-loop stops do.
\end{enumerate}
Refuted hypotheses are reported as such, and analyses run after the
pre-registered verdicts are labeled exploratory.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Conformal prediction for autonomous driving}
Conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005,angelopoulos2024book} constructs
prediction sets with a distribution-free, finite-sample marginal coverage
guarantee under exchangeability between calibration and test data, and
has been applied extensively to trajectory forecasting and to safe
planning under uncertainty~\cite{lindemann2024formal}. Recent work
calibrates trajectory-prediction uncertainty separately for each
scenario type~\cite{shu2025scenario}, feeds realized trajectories back
into the prediction region~\cite{wang2025fbcp}, certifies the clearance
of a selected trajectory with a CVaR margin~\cite{chang2026cvar}, and
couples online calibration with certified navigation in
crowds~\cite{guo2026coconav}. Lindemann~\emph{et al.}~\cite{lindemann2023}
embed conformal regions in a model predictive controller for
probabilistic safety, a guarantee that collapses when the deployment
distribution shifts, which Rahaman~\emph{et al.}~\cite{robustcp2026}
address with robust conformal prediction in a synthetic multi-agent
corridor. SafePath~\cite{safepath2025} guarantees that a set of
candidate paths from an LLM planner contains a safe one, in open-loop
(nuScenes) and closed-loop (synthetic traffic) tests. In these works the
guarantee is on the \emph{prediction} or on a candidate set, not on a
fallback-triggering decision evaluated on replayed driving logs under
reactive traffic. Ours is on the decision: the probability that a
minimum-risk maneuver fires before a harmful event, evaluated end to end
in closed loop.

\subsection{Online and decision-level conformal methods}
Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI)~\cite{aci2021} updates the
miscoverage level online so that long-run coverage holds under
arbitrary shift. Dixit~\emph{et al.}~\cite{dixit2023} use it inside a
model predictive controller to size a prediction set from delayed
observations, a guarantee on the region, not on a fallback decision.
Conformal Decision Theory (CDT)~\cite{cdt2024} carries the idea from
sets to decisions: it updates $\lambda_{t+1} = \lambda_t +
\eta(\varepsilon - \ell_t)$ so that the long-run average risk converges
to $\varepsilon$ at rate $O(1/t)$ without distributional assumptions,
demonstrated on robot navigation among pedestrians with a continuous
distance-based loss. Both give \emph{asymptotic, average} guarantees for
a quantity adjusted as data arrive. We target a one-shot, irreversible
trigger whose certificate must hold with finite samples before
deployment and whose errors include the harm that triggering causes. We
adopt both as baselines (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), applied
scenario by scenario.

Offline risk control now covers non-monotone
losses~\cite{angelopoulos2026nonmono}, several risks controlled at once
with priorities~\cite{multirisk2025}, and planning with control barrier
functions~\cite{gonzales2026}. Closest to our accounting of what the
fallback does, Joshi~\emph{et al.}~\cite{joshi2026post} certify the
outcome of switching from a baseline policy to a fallback policy, but
per instance and independently, with no notion of timing. An MRM trigger
is sequential and absorbing: \emph{when} it fires determines every
outcome after it.

\subsection{Task-relevant and closed-loop failure detection}
Farid~\emph{et al.}~\cite{taskrelevant2022} propose a
\emph{task-relevant} monitor that flags a misprediction only when the
resulting planning cost is anomalous, with false-positive and
false-negative bounds, evaluated open-loop and semi-reactively without
an actuated fallback. Their insight, that a task-irrelevant
misprediction should not count against a detector, is reflected in our
labeling: a collision suffered while stopped or struck from behind is an
\emph{induced-collision} cost of the fallback, never a missed
intervention.

Closest to our certification step, Luo~\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2022}
tune the threshold of a warning system with Mondrian conformal
prediction so that, among situations that are unsafe in the true future
\emph{in the absence of corrective action}, fewer than $\epsilon$ raise
no alert, using as few as $1/\epsilon$ unsafe examples. Their unsafe
label is a threshold on a safety score of the logged future, their
predictor is Trajectron++, and their driving experiments (nuScenes and
Lyft) score alerts against the logged data. The alert triggers nothing.
We adopt the same principle and do not claim it as new.
The differences that matter are the guarantee (class-conditional there,
marginal over scenarios here), the consequence of an alert (none there, an
executed maneuver here), and dataset shift and latency, which they do not
study (Supplementary Table~S1). We implement their certificate inside our
framework as a baseline (Supplementary Section~S1).

Sinha~\emph{et al.}~\cite{sinha2023} close the loop differently: a runtime
monitor calibrated with conformal prediction detects when perception
leaves the tolerance of a robust MPC controller that keeps a
perception-free fallback plan feasible, demonstrated on aircraft taxiing.
Their fallback is part of the controller design, whereas ours is an
irreversible maneuver whose trigger is the object of study.

Atoui~\emph{et al.}~\cite{atoui2025mrm} design and experimentally
validate an MRM fallback controller for sensor failure on highways,
taking the decision to engage it as given; we study that decision.
Scenario-based simulation is a standard route to evaluating automated
vehicle safety~\cite{sohrabi2021,xie2025}. Its conclusions need a
statistical foundation for stopping rules and risk
estimates~\cite{zhao2025stat}, and risk-based filtering can select
informative scenarios from large driving logs~\cite{puphal2025risk}. We
use replay of real logs with reactive traffic rather than synthetic
scenarios, and report every rate with a confidence interval.

\subsection{Risk-sensitive analysis and closed-loop infrastructure}
Chapman~\emph{et al.}~\cite{cvar2022} use Conditional Value-at-Risk to
assess the severity, not only the probability, of constraint violation;
we control a mean miss rate and leave a tail-severity extension to
future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). ScenarioNet~\cite{scenarionet2023}
unifies real driving logs (Argoverse~2, nuScenes, nuPlan, Lyft Level~5)
for interactive replay in MetaDrive with logged or reactive (IDM)
traffic. Every scenario in Section~\ref{sec:results} is a real logged
scenario replayed this way.

\section{Problem Formulation and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Fallback triggering as selective prediction}
\label{sec:formulation}
Consider a closed-loop rollout of a scenario $X$ under a fallback
policy governed by a scalar threshold $\lambda$: at every decision tick
the vehicle computes an uncertainty score $u_t(X)$ from a trajectory
predictor's output for nearby agents, and engages the minimum-risk
maneuver the first time $u_t(X) > \lambda$, remaining engaged for the
rest of the episode. Define the closed-loop loss
\begin{equation}
L(X,\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\, \text{harm in } X \text{ under }
\lambda,\ \text{MRM not first} \,\bigr],
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
the \emph{missed-intervention} indicator, and the complementary
over-conservatism quantity
\begin{equation}
S(X,\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\, \text{MRM fires under } \lambda,\
X \text{ harm-free at } \lambda=\infty \,\bigr],
\label{eq:stop}
\end{equation}
the \emph{unnecessary stop} indicator, evaluated against the
counterfactual rollout of the same scenario with the trigger disabled.
The goal is to choose $\hat\lambda$ such that
\begin{equation}
\Pr_{X_1,\dots,X_n}\Bigl(\, \mathbb{E}_X\bigl[L(X,\hat\lambda)\bigr] \le
\alpha \,\Bigr) \ge 1-\delta,
\label{eq:guarantee}
\end{equation}
while minimizing $\mathbb{E}_X[S(X,\hat\lambda)]$, the smallest
autonomy loss compatible with the risk target $\alpha$.

Let $h(X)$ be the first harm step of the reference run with the
fallback disabled ($\infty$ if none) and $\tau_\lambda(X)$ the firing
step. Because a run under the trigger coincides with the reference run
until it fires (Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}),
$L(X,\lambda)=\mathbb{1}[h(X)\le\tau_\lambda(X)]$ and
$S(X,\lambda)=\mathbb{1}[\tau_\lambda(X)<\infty,\ h(X)=\infty]$: both
alert-level indicators depend only on the reference run and the firing
step. The consequences of the fallback after it fires enter through
separate quantities, which we report but do not certify: residual harm
(harm anywhere in the executed run), induced collisions, and route
completion. Residual harm is not monotone in $\lambda$, since firing
earlier can itself create harm, and it includes harm after the alert, so
\eqref{eq:guarantee} does not bound it. Certifying it directly was not
achievable at a useful operating point (Supplementary Section~S5). Since the running
maximum of the score is non-decreasing, $\tau_\lambda$ is non-decreasing
in $\lambda$ and $L(X,\lambda)$ is non-decreasing in $\lambda$ for every
scenario. Equation~\eqref{eq:guarantee} is therefore a monotone-risk
statement, to which Conformal Risk Control~\cite{crc2022} applies
directly, but with an expectation guarantee. It bounds the calibration-averaged
risk, not the probability that a given calibrated threshold exceeds
$\alpha$. We use Learn-then-Test~\cite{ltt2021} for the
high-probability form. For a finite grid $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 >
\cdots > \lambda_J$ ordered from least to most conservative, we compute
the empirical risk $\hat R(\lambda_j) = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i L(X_i,\lambda_j)$
on $n$ calibration scenarios and a Hoeffding--Bentkus $p$-value for the
null hypothesis $H_j: \mathbb{E}[L(\cdot,\lambda_j)] > \alpha$, then apply
fixed-sequence testing starting from the most conservative $\lambda$.
Every $\lambda_j$ whose null is rejected satisfies
\eqref{eq:guarantee} simultaneously, with family-wise error controlled
at $\delta$, and $\hat\lambda$ is the least conservative such $\lambda_j$
(largest autonomy). The guarantee is therefore about the miss rate of the
alert, over scenarios exchangeable with the calibration scenarios, with
confidence $1-\delta$ over the calibration draw. We report the violation
frequency of both procedures over resplits rather than assuming it.

The marginal target is weaker than it may sound. With harm in a
fraction $\pi$ of scenarios, a marginal miss probability $\alpha$
leaves a fraction $\alpha/\pi$ of harmful scenarios unalerted, which is
$24\,\%$ at $\alpha=0.05$ and $\pi=0.158$ in our data
(Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}).

\subsection{Trigger score}
\label{sec:trigger-score}
At each decision tick the trigger score $u_t$ measures how far a
predicted future position of any nearby agent intrudes into the ego
vehicle's own near-term extent. Write the ego's constant-heading,
constant-speed extrapolation over a horizon of $T$ steps as
$\{p^{\mathrm{ego}}_\tau\}_{\tau=1}^{T}$. This is the same
route-agnostic motion model used by the harm metric in
Section~\ref{sec:harm}, deliberately \emph{not} the vehicle's planned,
route-following path, so the trigger cannot credit itself for
steering behavior the safety label does not assume. Write a
predictor's $K$-mode forecast for agent $i$ as
$\{\hat p^{(i,k)}_\tau\}$. Each predicted offset is decomposed into a
longitudinal component $a^{(i,k)}_\tau$ (along the ego heading) and a
lateral component $\ell^{(i,k)}_\tau$ (perpendicular to it), each
compared against a separate margin: a length-based longitudinal margin
$m^{\mathrm{lon}}_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(L_{\mathrm{ego}}+L_i)+c_{\mathrm{lon}}$
and a width-based lateral margin
$m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(W_{\mathrm{ego}}+W_i)+c_{\mathrm{lat}}$.
The intrusion at $(i,k,\tau)$ is
\begin{equation}
\rho^{(i,k)}_\tau = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \sqrt{
\left(\frac{a^{(i,k)}_\tau}{m^{\mathrm{lon}}_i}\right)^{\!2} +
\left(\frac{\ell^{(i,k)}_\tau}{m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i}\right)^{\!2}}\;
\right) m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
and $u_t = \max_{i,k,\tau} \rho^{(i,k)}_\tau$. A single isotropic
clearance radius cannot represent both margins at once: a radius set by
width alone risks missing a straight-ahead lead vehicle, and one set by
the half-diagonal risks triggering on adjacent-lane traffic. The
decomposition in \eqref{eq:score} is therefore a modeling choice made
during development on this reasoning. Our ablation
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) finds that it gives no measurable benefit
over a width-only isotropic radius, so we report it as a design
option rather than a requirement.

\subsection{Fallback maneuver}
\label{sec:mrm}
On triggering, the vehicle executes the minimum-risk maneuver defined
by UN Regulation No.~157 (Automated Lane Keeping Systems)
\S\,5.5.1~\cite{unr157}: decelerate inside the current lane with a
deceleration demand not exceeding $4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, lane-keeping
control otherwise unchanged, holding the vehicle stationary once
stopped. This is enforced by a proportional controller tracking the
target speed profile $v(t) = \max(v_0 - 4.0\,t,\,0)$ from the moment of
triggering.

\subsection{Certification under covariate shift}
\label{sec:shift}
The certificate in \eqref{eq:guarantee} assumes calibration and
deployment scenarios are exchangeable. When the deployment distribution
differs by covariate shift, we reweight calibration scenarios by an
estimated density ratio $w(x) \propto p_{\mathrm{target}}(x) /
p_{\mathrm{source}}(x)$ computed from pre-deployment scenario covariates
only (agent counts and speeds, initial gap, route length). A
probabilistic domain classifier is trained on unlabeled target
scenarios, following weighted conformal prediction~\cite{tibshirani2019}.
The weighted empirical risk $\hat R_w(\lambda) = \sum_i w_i L(X_i,\lambda)
/ \sum_i w_i$ is tested with the Hoeffding--Bentkus $p$-value at the
Kish effective sample size $n_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i
w_i^2$. As the weights concentrate, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ shrinks and the
test certifies a more conservative threshold, and eventually none at
all. The procedure thus reports when the source data cannot support a
guarantee for the target, rather than asserting one that has silently
expired.

\subsection{Evaluating every trigger by lookup}
\label{sec:exact-eval}
Fix a scenario $X$ and its random seed. The \emph{reference run}
$R(X)$ disables the fallback. For a decision tick $j$, the \emph{forced
run} $F(X,j)$ is identical to $R(X)$ until tick $j$ and then executes the
MRM for the rest of the episode. Let $\Phi(X,j)$ denote any outcome of
$F(X,j)$ (miss, stop, induced collision, residual harm, route completion)
and $\Phi(X,\infty)$ the outcome of $R(X)$. Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}
summarizes the procedure.

\begin{observation}[Firing-tick sufficiency]
\label{prop:exact}
Assume (A1) the simulator, background traffic and predictor are
deterministic functions of the seed and of the ego's trajectory, so two
runs with the same seed and the same ego control up to a step have
identical states up to that step; (A2) the trigger's decision at tick $j$
depends only on the state history up to tick $j$; (A3) once the fallback
fires it is absorbing, and the control after firing depends only on the
state at firing. Then for every trigger with firing tick
$\tau(X)\in\{0,\Delta,2\Delta,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}$, the run under the trigger is
$F(X,\tau(X))$, and every outcome equals $\Phi(X,\tau(X))$. This holds
for any score, threshold, online update rule and actuation latency
$\ell$ (for which the run is $F(X,\tau(X)+\ell)$).
\end{observation}
\begin{IEEEproof}
By induction over steps. Until $\tau$ the trigger has not fired, so by
(A3) the control is that of $R(X)$ and by (A1) the states equal those of
$R(X)$; by (A2) the trigger's decision at each such tick is a function of
these states, hence the same in every run sharing them, so $\tau$ is
determined by $R(X)$ alone. At $\tau$ the fallback engages with a control
that depends only on the state at $\tau$ (A3), which is the control of
$F(X,\tau)$. Both runs then have equal states by (A1).
\end{IEEEproof}
Two corollaries follow. First, alert-level indicators need no forced run:
$L$ and $S$ are functions of $R(X)$ and $\tau$ alone. Second, the
consequences of the fallback need $n_{\text{ticks}}$ forced rollouts per
scenario (16.8 on average, at most 26, on the Argoverse~2 test split),
after which any number of triggers is evaluated by lookup, where direct
execution needs one rollout per trigger, threshold and latency (over
7{,}000 per scenario here). The observation is elementary and its value
is practical: an exhaustive, paired comparison becomes affordable.
Assumption (A1) is an idealization, so we measure its violation. Over the
first 33{,}761 forced rollouts (2{,}000 scenarios), 98.4\,\% agreed with
the reference run to within 1\,cm before firing, the pre-fire
time-to-collision trace differed in 1.2\,\%, and the pre-fire harm status
in one rollout (0.003\,\%). We report every analysis on all scenarios and
after dropping the 3.6\,\% with any flagged drift. We also executed four
triggers directly, live and without replay, on 61 random test scenarios
(244 scenario--trigger pairs, one trigger with a one-tick latency).
Firing step, miss, unnecessary stop and induced collision agreed with the
lookup in all 244 pairs, and harm anywhere in the run in all but one
(0.4\,\%, [0.1, 2.3]); route completion differed by at most 0.018
(Supplementary Section~S7). The lookup is exact only under (A1), but it
matched direct execution within this tolerance.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Simulation environment}
All rollouts are executed headless in MetaDrive, replaying scenarios
converted by ScenarioNet~\cite{scenarionet2023} from Argoverse~2 Motion
Forecasting~\cite{av2motion2021} and nuScenes~\cite{nuscenes2020}.
Non-ego traffic is controlled reactively by the Intelligent Driver
Model~\cite{idm2000}, not replayed open-loop from the log. Pure log
replay roughly tripled the no-fallback collision rate on a 1{,}000-scenario
diagnostic sample, because logged agents cannot react to an ego vehicle
that deviates from its log, precisely what a fallback maneuver produces,
and would misrepresent the cost of stopping.

\subsection{Harm labeling}
\label{sec:harm}
A rollout is labeled harmful using the nuPlan closed-loop safety
definition~\cite{nuplan2024}: an at-fault collision, or a
time-to-collision below $0.95\,\mathrm{s}$ computed over a $3.0\,$s
horizon at $0.1\,$s resolution, not evaluated while the ego is
stationary or against tracks behind it. Fault attribution follows the
nuPlan convention. A collision the ego suffers while stationary, or while
struck from behind, is not at-fault and never counts as a missed
intervention. It is recorded as an \emph{induced collision}, the
measurable cost of a false or premature trigger.

\subsection{Predictor}
The trajectory predictor is AutoBot~\cite{autobot2022}, one of many
learned multi-modal forecasters~\cite{shi2025survey} (6 modes, 2.1\,s
history, 6\,s horizon), trained with the UniTraj framework~\cite{unitraj2024}
on the Argoverse~2 Motion Forecasting training split (minADE$_6$ = 1.09\,m
on held-out validation scenes). It runs online inside the closed loop, so
its input is the actual, possibly ego-deviated, simulator history, with
the UniTraj preprocessing unchanged. Vehicles are predicted by the
network. Pedestrians and cyclists, which the checkpoint was not trained
on, receive constant-velocity forecasts.

\subsection{Datasets and splits}
\label{sec:data}
All scenarios come from Argoverse~2 validation, disjoint from the
predictor's training data. They are split deterministically by scenario
identifier into development (14{,}990), calibration (4{,}971) and test
(5{,}027) sets, and design choices were made on the development split
only. The shifted target is nuScenes (9{,}041 scenarios of 8.1\,s, Boston
and Singapore, versus Argoverse~2's six U.S. cities). Scenarios in which
the ego moves less than 10\,m are excluded, as in ScenarioNet. The
pre-registered evaluation uses 2{,}000 calibration, 2{,}000 test and
1{,}500 nuScenes scenarios. Ten Argoverse~2 scenarios (six calibration,
four test) whose per-agent records could not be matched after a
re-scoring pass are dropped from every analysis, leaving 1{,}994
calibration, 1{,}996 test and 1{,}504 nuScenes scenarios. A second shifted
target, 1{,}000 Waymo Open Motion~\cite{waymo2021} validation scenarios
(9.1\,s, 1\,s of history), was collected under a separate pre-registered
amendment (Section~\ref{sec:res-waymo}). Decisions are taken at 2\,Hz,
with identical miss rates at 2 and 10\,Hz on a paired development
comparison and a 10\,Hz replication on 100 test scenarios.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
All methods use the same predictor, the same MRM and the same test
scenarios, and are evaluated by lookup (Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{T1 --- confidence threshold} (deployed practice): fire when
$1-\max_k p_k$ of the network-predicted vehicle whose predicted modes come
closest to the ego exceeds a threshold. Agents the network does not
predict (pedestrians, cyclists, distant vehicles) are excluded from this
score. The threshold is tuned on calibration data to the most permissive
value with empirical miss rate $\le\alpha$. A variant that uses the
nearest agent ahead is also evaluated.
\item \textbf{T2 --- ensemble threshold}: the same tuning, applied to the
spread of endpoint predictions across five Monte Carlo dropout passes
\cite{galghahramani2016}.
\item \textbf{T3 --- open-loop conformal}: inflate every predicted mode by
the split-conformal radius of the predictor's 3\,s error on held-out
Argoverse~2 logs at level $\alpha$~\cite{vovk2005}, and fire when the
inflated region touches the ego. This is the guarantee-on-the-prediction
design that the paper argues is insufficient.
\item \textbf{Tuned geometric}: our score with an empirically tuned
threshold and no certificate. It isolates the contribution of the
calibrator.
\item \textbf{CDT}~\cite{cdt2024} and \textbf{ACI}~\cite{aci2021}: online
decision- and prediction-level adaptation, updated after every
scenario. Each uses the step size that performs best on test, which
favors the baselines.
\item \textbf{Oracle}: the best threshold in hindsight on test data.
It is not deployable and is reported as an upper bound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
For every method we report the missed-intervention, unnecessary-stop and
fallback-induced-collision rates, autonomy (the fraction of scenarios
completed without the fallback) and route completion, with Wilson 95\,\%
intervals. Validity of each certificate is assessed over 200 random
half-and-half resplits, without replacement, of the pooled 3{,}990
calibration and test scenarios. A valid certificate keeps the frequency
of test miss rate above $\alpha$ at or below $\delta$. The resplits
overlap, so they are not independent draws: the frequency estimates the
violation probability for this pool, with Monte Carlo error (Wilson
95\,\% interval [6.2, 14.4] for 9.5\,\%, [36.8, 50.4] for 43.5\,\%), and
the test half's own miss rate has a standard error of about 0.5 points.
Scenarios from one recording may be dependent. Only the nuScenes
identifiers expose a grouping (Section~\ref{sec:res-shift}).
Harm is scored with the single nuPlan definition above.
Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} repeats the key comparison under three
alternative harm definitions.

\subsection{Pre-registration and reproducibility}
\label{sec:prereg}
The harm definition, $\alpha = 0.05$, $\delta = 0.10$, and the refutation
criteria for three hypotheses were recorded in a version-controlled note
before any calibration or test data were collected: \textbf{H1}, that
open-loop metrics rank triggers differently from closed-loop outcomes;
\textbf{H2}, that a certified trigger achieves a better
safety--conservatism trade-off than a tuned fixed threshold; and
\textbf{H3}, that a threshold certified on Argoverse~2 loses its
guarantee on nuScenes. A later amendment, written before any Waymo
outcome existed, added \textbf{H3-W}, the same hypothesis on Waymo, and
a few-label recalibration study. The note carries further dated
amendments (a corrected confidence baseline, the exactness measurements,
outcome-level certification, robustness checks and a direct-execution
check), each written before the corresponding outcomes were evaluated,
and it labels the analyses run afterwards as post hoc.

\emph{Data and code availability.} Code, the pre-registration note with
all amendments, configuration files and per-scenario rollout records are
available at \url{https://github.com/hwaqas160/paper03-risk-fallback}
and archived at \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23054382}. Each
scenario's record contains every score trace and outcome, so every table
can be regenerated without re-simulation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All results use $\alpha = 0.05$ and $\delta = 0.10$, 1{,}994 calibration
and 1{,}996 test scenarios from Argoverse~2, and 1{,}504 nuScenes
scenarios, with Wilson 95\,\% intervals unless stated. Dropping the
3.6\,\% of scenarios with any flagged simulator drift changes no
conclusion below. Sections~\ref{sec:res-main}--\ref{sec:res-shift} are
the pre-registered analyses. Of the subsections that follow, the
Waymo recalibration (R1--R4) is pre-registered; the consequences of the
intervention, the class-conditional comparison, the reverse shift,
few-label recalibration and the robustness checks
(Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}) are post hoc or partly so, and are
labeled. Full details of the exploratory analyses are in the
Supplementary Material.

\subsection{Does a certificate keep its promise, and what does it cost? (RQ1)}
\label{sec:res-main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives every method at its operating point on the
Argoverse~2 test set. The certified trigger (LTT) misses 3.7\,\% of
harmful events [95\,\% CI 2.9--4.6], below the 5\,\% target, with
28.5\,\% unnecessary stops and 2.6\,\% fallback-induced collisions.
The last column repeats the calibration on 200 random resplits and
reports how often the realized test miss rate exceeds $\alpha$. A valid
certificate keeps this at or below $\delta = 10\,\%$. LTT does (9.5\,\%).
The same geometric score with a threshold tuned empirically, and its
Conformal Risk Control (CRC) counterpart, which guarantees only an
expectation, exceed the target in 43.5\,\% and 41.0\,\% of resplits, and
the tuned confidence trigger in 50.5\,\%. Certification is therefore the
only design here that keeps its target reliably.
Fig.~\ref{fig:operating-points} plots every method against the
geometric score's own curve as its threshold varies, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:validity} shows the resplit distributions behind the
violation column. CRC and empirical tuning
select the same threshold on this calibration set. CRC requires
$\hat R \le \alpha - (1-\alpha)/n = 4.95\,\%$ instead of $5\,\%$, and the
calibration risk jumps from 4.81\,\% to 5.02\,\% between adjacent grid
points, so no threshold falls in between.

\subsection{Do open-loop metrics choose the closed-loop trigger? (RQ2)}
\label{sec:res-h1}
Table~\ref{tab:h1} ranks six trigger scores by open-loop AUROC of their
scenario peak for predicting harm, and by closed-loop unnecessary stops
at each score's certified threshold. Rankings agree at the top and
diverge below it: Kendall's $\tau = 0.60$ on Argoverse~2, $0.47$ on
nuScenes and $0.73$ on Waymo. The top-ranked score is the same on
Argoverse~2 and on Waymo, and differs on nuScenes by only 0.2 points of
stops. The pre-registered rule refutes H1 only if $\tau \ge 0.8$ and the
top score coincides in every setting, so H1 is not refuted, but the
evidence is weak. The clearest disagreement is at the bottom: the

confidence-based uncertainty is on average associated with \emph{less}
harm, yet a threshold on it still reaches the miss target, at 75.8\,\%
stops, by firing indiscriminately.

The Argoverse~2 certificate does not transfer. On nuScenes the threshold

hypothesis H3 is \textbf{supported} (15.2\,\% after dropping drift
scenarios). Part of this is a floor rather than shift: 5.0\,\% of
nuScenes scenarios already contain harm at the first decision tick, which
even a trigger that always fires cannot prevent (0.1\,\% on
Argoverse~2). That floor equals the target, so no threshold can be
certified at $\alpha = 0.05$ on nuScenes. With those scenarios excluded
(post hoc), the shifted miss rate is 9.2\,\% [7.8, 10.8], still above the

under the larger measured shift and held under the smaller, but two
targets cannot establish a trend.

doubles unnecessary stops (26.1\,\% to 52.4\,\%). On the avoidable subset
it reaches 2.2\,\%, below the target, but its effective sample size is only
68, so the certificate is achieved almost entirely through conservatism
(54.5\,\% stops).

\subsection{What the intervention does, and a class-conditional certificate (post hoc)}
\label{sec:res-cost}
The certified quantity is the alert. Table~\ref{tab:cost} partitions every
scenario by what happens after the fallback fires. At the certified
threshold on Argoverse~2, 3.7\,\% of scenarios are missed, in a further
1.7\,\% the fallback fires before harm but harm still occurs, and in
9.8\,\% harm is prevented. In a further 1.1\,\% of all scenarios, which
are harmless without a fallback, the intervened run contains harm (nuPlan
definition), and 2.6\,\% suffer an induced collision. Harm in the intervened run is
therefore 6.5\,\% (15.1\,\% without a fallback), almost twice the
certified miss rate. On the fresh replication split it is 5.0\,\% [3.7,
6.6] against 3.4\,\% missed. A trigger that always fires creates harm in
3.1\,\% of scenarios. On nuScenes the same threshold leaves 15.6\,\%
residual harm. Induced collisions are other vehicles striking the stopped
ego, and concentrate where the fallback fires early: 9.2\,\% at or before
the median firing tick against 3.0\,\% later (Supplementary
Section~S6). The fallback removes a large share of harm, but the
certificate does not speak to what remains, and certifying residual harm
directly at 5\,\% required firing in nearly every scenario and still
failed validity (Supplementary Section~S5).

\emph{Reading a 5\,\% marginal target.} As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:formulation}, 24\,\% of harmful scenarios go unalerted
(58\,\% on nuScenes).

\emph{Class-conditional certification and reverse shift.} A Luo-style
class-conditional certificate at $\epsilon=0.05$ selects a threshold
equivalent to always firing (84.9\,\% unnecessary stops). At the
marginal-matched $\epsilon=0.32$ its conditional miss rate on nuScenes is
66\,\% against its 32\,\% target, so it does not survive the shift either
(Supplementary Material, Section~S1 and Table~S1). Certifying on
nuScenes and deploying on Argoverse~2 transfers safely but
conservatively (miss 1.9--2.0\,\%, Section~S2), a single-pair
observation.

\subsection{Second shifted dataset and recalibration}
\label{sec:res-waymo}
\emph{Waymo Open Motion.} Table~\ref{tab:shift} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:shift-latency}a deploy the same
Argoverse~2-certified threshold, unchanged, on 1{,}000 Waymo Open Motion
validation scenarios~\cite{waymo2021} (9.1\,s, 1\,s of history). It misses 2.7\,\% [1.9, 3.9], below the
5\,\% target. Hypothesis H3-W was pre-registered with the expectation
that this interval would exclude $\alpha$ from above, as on nuScenes; it
does exclude $\alpha$, but from below, so H3-W is \textbf{refuted}. Taken
with H3, the guarantee's failure under shift is dataset-dependent, not
universal: the same certified threshold fails on nuScenes and holds,
conservatively, on Waymo. A density-ratio-weighted repair, applied for
consistency even though nothing here needed repairing, lowers the miss
rate further to 1.2\,\% [0.7, 2.1] at a small increase in stops (45.1\,\%
to 50.2\,\%).

\emph{Waymo recalibration (pre-registered R1--R4).} We draw $k$ labeled
Waymo scenes at random (200 draws) and evaluate on the rest, a design
pre-registered before any Waymo outcome was read (full table and the
exploratory nuScenes counterpart in Supplementary Section~S3). Uncertified tuning violates the target in
69.5\,\% and 59\,\% of draws with 40 and 60 target scenes (\textbf{R1
holds}: violation $\ge 50\,\%$ at both). Weighting the Argoverse~2
calibration set with 60 Waymo scenes ($n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 232$) keeps
validity (0\,\% violation, \textbf{R2 holds}) at a cost of 21.3 points
of unnecessary stops over the in-domain rate (\textbf{R3 holds}).
\textbf{R4}, which restates H3-W, is refuted for the same reason: the
source certificate already holds on Waymo, so there was nothing to
repair. Few labeled target scenes are therefore dangerous without a
certificate even when the source certificate transfers.

\emph{Sensitivity.} Non-reactive log-replay traffic, a 2.0\,m/s$^2$
comfort deceleration and 10\,Hz decisions, each paired by scenario seed
against the main run, leave every conclusion above unchanged. The
certified miss rate stays below the target in all three, and only log
replay raises unnecessary stops, by 7.9 points, through a higher harm
base rate (Supplementary Section~S4).

\subsection{Robustness checks (post hoc)}

Two objections are addressed directly, on data not used anywhere above.
Both were pre-registered as post hoc robustness checks before running.

confidence and ensemble scores is unfair by construction.

\emph{Does validity replicate on unseen data and a second predictor?}
We recalibrate LTT on 904 fresh Argoverse~2 scenarios, disjoint from

uncertified baselines), and its miss rate stays below $\alpha$
(Supplementary Section~S8).

\section{Scope and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{What we claim, and what we do not.} We claim that certification is
what makes a closed-loop trigger keep its stated alert-level miss rate,
that it costs a measurable number of unnecessary stops, and that the
guarantee is fragile to dataset shift and to latency. We do not claim a
better-performing trigger, since a tuned threshold and an online method
stop less often and the pre-registered hypothesis that said otherwise is
refuted, and we do not claim a guarantee on the outcome.

\emph{Alert versus outcome.} The certificate bounds the probability, over
scenarios, that harm is not pre-empted by an alert. That is the quantity
a deployment authority can audit, but it is weaker than a bound among
harmful scenarios, and it bounds the alert, not the outcome: harm still
occurs after some alerts and is sometimes created by the intervention.
Certifying residual harm directly was not achievable with this score and
maneuver at a useful operating point (Supplementary Section~S5), so it
calls for a different maneuver or score rather than a different
calibrator. The 5.0\,\% first-tick floor on nuScenes likewise shows that
a miss metric on harm anywhere in a replayed snippet mixes preventable
events with events already under way. We propose the avoidable-only
variant for future benchmarks.

\emph{Simulator, predictor and perception.} All results use MetaDrive
with rule-based (IDM) agents, and whether the ordering of triggers
survives learned agent behavior is open. LTT's validity replicates with a
second predictor (Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}), and so does the gap to
the tuned confidence trigger (30.6\,\% against 68.3\,\% stops). Only the
ensemble and open-loop comparisons, which need dropout passes and error
files this arm lacks, were not repeated. The
predictor receives agent states, which isolates the triggering decision
from perception error. Composing the certificate with a calibrated
perception module is future work.

\emph{Shift and dependence.} The weighted certificate covers shift in
observable scenario covariates, and its effective sample size (68 in our
nuScenes subset) shows when it becomes unreliable. Every guarantee also
treats scenarios as exchangeable, which scenarios from one recording may
violate. Our nuScenes samples span only 37 and 23 scenes, and group-level
resplits were not possible for the other datasets.

\emph{Harm definition and fallback family.} The geometric score's
advantage holds under three time-to-collision thresholds and a
collision-only definition (Section~\ref{sec:res-robust}). Fault
attribution is not varied, and a severity-weighted or tail (CVaR)
certificate~\cite{cvar2022} requires only a change of loss. We evaluate
in-lane stops at the UN~R157 deceleration limit. Other fallbacks, such as
a lateral pull-over, change the quantities priced but not the method.

%=======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
An alert-level guarantee on a fallback trigger is not a permanent
property of the model. In the setting studied, it depends on the data it
was certified on, the timing of the intervention, and what the fallback
itself does. Evaluated with the fallback executed, a Learn-then-Test
certified trigger keeps its 5\,\% target on the calibration distribution
(3.7\,\% missed, violations in 9.5\,\% of resplits against 43.5\,\%
without a certificate) at a cost of about 2.5 points of unnecessary
stops, and deployed-practice confidence and ensemble triggers need
68--71\,\% stops for similar safety. The certificate controls the alert,
not the outcome: harm in the intervened run is 6.5\,\%, 1.1\,\% of it
created by the intervention, and a 5\,\% marginal target leaves about a
quarter of harmful scenarios unalerted. The same certificate fails on
nuScenes (13.2\,\% missed, with wide scene-level uncertainty) but holds,
conservatively, on Waymo (2.7\,\% missed), so failure under shift is
dataset-dependent. One decision tick of latency breaks it, and it can be
unreachable when the target data contain harm no trigger can prevent.
These findings argue, for the setting studied, for treating
certification, shift monitoring and latency as one audited quantity, and
the lookup protocol makes that audit inexpensive to repeat for any
trigger.
