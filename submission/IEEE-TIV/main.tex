\begin{abstract}
A fallback trigger decides when an automated vehicle should abandon its
task for a minimum-risk maneuver. Statistical certificates for such
triggers bound the alert, not what happens once the maneuver executes.
Because a rollout depends only on the tick at which the fallback fires,
one reference run plus one forced rollout per decision tick evaluates
every trigger, threshold and latency by lookup, checked against direct
execution. On replayed Argoverse~2, nuScenes and Waymo logs with reactive
traffic, we certify the alert with Learn-then-Test and the outcome, the
harm in the run actually driven, with risk-ordered fixed-sequence
testing. The alert certificate keeps its 5\,\% target (3.7\,\% missed,
violations in 9.5\,\% of resplits against 43.5\,\% uncertified) but leaves
6.5\,\% harm after the fallback. Outcome certification at a 7.5\,\% target
realizes 5.4\,\% harm. At that target a time-to-collision trigger is cheapest at zero latency (10\,\% unnecessary stops) but needs 85\,\% at 0.5\,s of latency, where a learned counterfactual trigger needs 26\,\% against 38\,\%. At the alert level, confidence and ensemble triggers need 68--71\,\%. Certificates
fail on nuScenes and in two of six held-out cities but hold on Waymo.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} vehicles that lack confidence in their own
situational understanding are expected to fall back on a minimum-risk
maneuver (MRM): slow to a stop, pull to the shoulder, or hand control
back to a human. When to invoke it is, in most deployed and published
systems, a hand-tuned threshold on a scalar confidence, uncertainty or risk
score from a perception or prediction module. Such a threshold carries
no guarantee, and learned uncertainty estimates are known to degrade
under the shift a vehicle meets in a new city or weather. The failure
is silent: the system keeps reporting a confidence number that is no
longer right.

Conformal prediction offers a principled alternative, a
distribution-free, finite-sample guarantee under exchangeability, and
has been applied widely to trajectory forecasting and planning in
driving. A guarantee on a prediction, or on a warning, is nevertheless
not a guarantee on the consequences of the action it triggers. Whether
an alert leads to a fallback is rarely evaluated in closed loop, and
triggering is not free: a vehicle that stops in a live lane because its
uncertainty grew is not obviously safer than one that continues, and a
trigger that fires whenever asked trivially achieves a good miss rate
while being useless. Reporting only coverage, or only collision rate,
hides this trade-off.

We ask a narrower question than ``which trigger is best'':
\emph{can the outcome of a fallback trigger, not only its alert, be
certified, and how does that certificate behave under actuation latency
and dataset shift?}
Certifying a threshold on a risk score against missed events is not new.
Luo~\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2022} do so with conformal prediction, and
Conformal Decision Theory~\cite{cdt2024} calibrates decisions through a
long-run \emph{average} guarantee. We build on these and do not claim the
certification step. An MRM trigger nevertheless differs in kind: it is an
irreversible, one-shot decision, its errors are paid in two currencies
(missed interventions, and the harm of stopping itself), and a deployed
vehicle needs its guarantee before the first mile. To our knowledge, no
prior work certifies a fallback trigger and then measures, in closed
loop, what the executed fallback does.

We organize the study around four research questions, each tied to a
hypothesis fixed before data collection (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).
\textbf{RQ1 (H2):} does a certificate keep its promise on the
closed-loop outcome, and what does it cost in unnecessary stops and
induced collisions? \textbf{RQ2 (H1):} do open-loop metrics choose the
trigger that closed-loop safety does? \textbf{RQ3 (H3, H3-W):} what
happens to a certificate under cross-dataset shift, and how much can
unlabeled or a few labeled target scenarios repair? \textbf{RQ4:} how
sensitive is it to the timing of the intervention, and can the outcome
be certified under latency? RQ4's outcome-level part was added by
later amendments and is labeled as such.

We make four contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A lookup evaluation protocol for executed fallbacks.}
Under a determinism assumption whose violation we measure, a rollout's
outcome depends only on \emph{when} the fallback fires
(Observation~\ref{prop:exact}, Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}). One reference run
plus one rollout forced to fire at each decision tick (about 17 per
scenario) evaluates every trigger, threshold, online method and latency
on identical physics, where direct execution would need over 7{,}000
rollouts per scenario here. We check the lookup against direct execution
(Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}).
\item \textbf{Outcome-level certification.} Risk-ordered
fixed-sequence testing certifies the harm in the run the vehicle actually
drives, a non-monotone loss that the alert-level certificate does not
bound (it leaves 6.5\,\% harm after the fallback). At a 7.5\,\% target the
certified threshold realizes 5.4\,\% harm with a valid violation frequency,
and the same data give an audited upper bound on outcome harm.
\item \textbf{A map of which trigger wins when.} A certified
time-to-collision (TTC) trigger is cheapest at zero latency and
the predictor-based triggers are far more expensive, so under a
TTC-defined harm label a learned predictor adds nothing there. At 0.5\,s
of actuation latency the TTC trigger needs 85\,\% unnecessary stops and a
learned counterfactual trigger, trained on counterfactual safe firing
windows from the lookup table, needs 26\,\%. Confidence and ensemble
triggers, the uncertainty-based practice, need 68--71\,\% at the alert level.
\item \textbf{A failure map for the certificate.} An Argoverse~2
certificate fails on nuScenes and in two of six held-out cities but holds
on Waymo, with failures tracking the target's harm rate. A
noise-corrected violation measure separates methods that are clearly
violated from those that are only near the boundary, and spatial
dependence weakens the high-probability statement in practice.
\end{enumerate}
Refuted hypotheses are reported as such, and analyses run after the
pre-registered verdicts are labeled exploratory.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Conformal prediction for autonomous driving}
Conformal prediction~\cite{vovk2005,angelopoulos2024book} gives prediction sets a distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantee under exchangeability and has been applied widely to trajectory forecasting and safe planning~\cite{lindemann2024formal}: per scenario type~\cite{shu2025scenario}, with feedback from realized trajectories~\cite{wang2025fbcp}, with CVaR clearance margins~\cite{chang2026cvar}, and in crowd navigation~\cite{guo2026coconav}. Lindemann \emph{et al.}~\cite{lindemann2023} embed conformal regions in a model predictive controller, a guarantee that collapses under shift, which Rahaman \emph{et al.}~\cite{robustcp2026} address with robust conformal prediction; SafePath~\cite{safepath2025} guarantees that a set of LLM-planned paths contains a safe one. In these works the guarantee is on the \emph{prediction} or a candidate set, not on a fallback-triggering decision replayed on driving logs under reactive traffic. Ours is on the decision, and in Section~\ref{sec:outcome} on its executed outcome.

\subsection{Online and decision-level conformal methods}
Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI)~\cite{aci2021} updates the
miscoverage level online, and Dixit~\emph{et al.}~\cite{dixit2023} use it
inside a model predictive controller to size a prediction set, a guarantee
on the region, not on a fallback decision. Conformal Decision Theory
(CDT)~\cite{cdt2024} carries the idea to decisions: it updates
$\lambda_{t+1} = \lambda_t + \eta(\varepsilon - \ell_t)$ so that the
long-run average risk converges to $\varepsilon$, demonstrated on robot
navigation with a continuous distance-based loss. Both give
\emph{asymptotic, average} guarantees. We target a one-shot, irreversible
trigger whose certificate must hold with finite samples before deployment,
and adopt both as baselines (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). Offline risk
control now covers non-monotone losses~\cite{angelopoulos2026nonmono},
several risks at once~\cite{multirisk2025}, and planning with control
barrier functions~\cite{gonzales2026}. Closest to our accounting of what
the fallback does, Joshi~\emph{et al.}~\cite{joshi2026post} certify the
outcome of switching to a fallback policy, per instance and with no notion
of timing. An MRM trigger is sequential and absorbing: \emph{when} it fires
determines every outcome after it.

\subsection{Task-relevant and closed-loop failure detection}
Farid~\emph{et al.}~\cite{taskrelevant2022} propose a
\emph{task-relevant} monitor that flags a misprediction only when the
resulting planning cost is anomalous, evaluated open-loop and
semi-reactively without an actuated fallback. Their insight is reflected
in our labeling: a collision suffered while stopped or struck from behind is
an \emph{induced-collision} cost of the fallback, never a missed
intervention.

Closest to our certification step, Luo~\emph{et al.}~\cite{luo2022} tune
the threshold of a warning system with Mondrian conformal prediction so
that, among situations that are unsafe in the true future \emph{in the
absence of corrective action}, fewer than $\epsilon$ raise no alert.
Their predictor is Trajectron++ and their alerts are scored against logged
data, so the alert triggers nothing. We adopt the same principle and do not
claim it as new. The differences that matter are the guarantee
(class-conditional there, marginal over scenarios here), the consequence of
an alert (none there, an executed maneuver here), and dataset shift and
latency, which they do not study (Supplementary Table~S1). We implement
their certificate as a baseline (Supplementary Section~S1).

Sinha~\emph{et al.}~\cite{sinha2023} close the loop differently: a
conformal runtime monitor detects when perception leaves the tolerance of a
robust MPC controller that keeps a perception-free fallback plan feasible.
Their fallback is part of the controller design, whereas ours is an
irreversible maneuver whose trigger is the object of study. Atoui and
Resende~\cite{atoui2025mrm} design and validate an MRM controller,
taking the decision to engage it as given. Scenario-based simulation is a
standard route to evaluating automated vehicle
safety~\cite{sohrabi2021,xie2025}, needs a statistical foundation for
stopping rules~\cite{zhao2025stat}, and can use risk-based
filtering~\cite{puphal2025risk}. We replay real logs with reactive traffic.

Fallback triggering also sits within established safety frameworks.
Runtime monitors that switch to a trusted fallback, as
in~\cite{sinha2023,atoui2025mrm}, follow the runtime-assurance idea of a
monitored learned component with a trusted backup. Switching policies
have been learned for this purpose, with safety from
reachability~\cite{miller2023rta}, but without a statistical certificate on
the executed outcome, and conformal abort alerts have been calibrated in
flight testing~\cite{feldman2025} without evaluating the response.
Responsibility-Sensitive Safety~\cite{rss2017} gives model-based rules for
when a vehicle is at fault, whereas our trigger is certified from data.
SOTIF~\cite{iso21448} and UL~4600~\cite{ul4600} frame assurance at the
system level, but do not specify how to certify a trigger threshold.
Selective prediction~\cite{geifman2017} studies when a model should abstain
at a controlled risk, the same reject-option structure as a trigger,
except that here abstention is an executed maneuver with its own cost. We
do not claim priority in any of these areas.

Chapman~\emph{et al.}~\cite{cvar2022} use Conditional Value-at-Risk to assess the severity of constraint violation, a tail extension we leave to future work. ScenarioNet~\cite{scenarionet2023} unifies real driving logs for interactive replay in MetaDrive, which is how every scenario is replayed here.

\section{Problem Formulation and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Fallback triggering as selective prediction}
\label{sec:formulation}
Consider a closed-loop rollout of a scenario $X$ under a fallback
policy governed by a scalar threshold $\lambda$: at every decision tick
the vehicle computes an uncertainty score $u_t(X)$ from a trajectory
predictor's output for nearby agents, and engages the minimum-risk
maneuver the first time $u_t(X) > \lambda$, remaining engaged for the
rest of the episode. Define the closed-loop loss
\begin{equation}
L(X,\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\, \text{harm in } X \text{ under }
\lambda,\ \text{MRM not first} \,\bigr],
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
the \emph{missed-intervention} indicator, and the complementary
over-conservatism quantity
\begin{equation}
S(X,\lambda) = \mathbb{1}\bigl[\, \text{MRM fires under } \lambda,\
X \text{ harm-free at } \lambda=\infty \,\bigr],
\label{eq:stop}
\end{equation}
the \emph{unnecessary stop} indicator, evaluated against the
counterfactual rollout of the same scenario with the trigger disabled.
The goal is to choose $\hat\lambda$ such that
\begin{equation}
\Pr_{X_1,\dots,X_n}\Bigl(\, \mathbb{E}_X\bigl[L(X,\hat\lambda)\bigr] \le
\alpha \,\Bigr) \ge 1-\delta,
\label{eq:guarantee}
\end{equation}
while minimizing $\mathbb{E}_X[S(X,\hat\lambda)]$, the smallest
autonomy loss compatible with the risk target $\alpha$.

Let $h(X)$ be the first harm step of the reference run with the
fallback disabled ($\infty$ if none) and $\tau_\lambda(X)$ the firing
step. Because a run under the trigger coincides with the reference run
until it fires (Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}),
$L(X,\lambda)=\mathbb{1}[h(X)\le\tau_\lambda(X)]$ and
$S(X,\lambda)=\mathbb{1}[\tau_\lambda(X)<\infty,\ h(X)=\infty]$: both
alert-level indicators depend only on the reference run and the firing
step. The consequences of the fallback after it fires enter through
separate quantities, which we report but do not certify: residual harm
(harm anywhere in the executed run), induced collisions, and route
completion. Residual harm is not monotone in $\lambda$, since firing
earlier can itself create harm, and it includes harm after the alert, so
\eqref{eq:guarantee} does not bound it. Section~\ref{sec:outcome} certifies
it directly. Since the running
maximum of the score is non-decreasing, $\tau_\lambda$ is non-decreasing
in $\lambda$ and $L(X,\lambda)$ is non-decreasing in $\lambda$ for every
scenario. Equation~\eqref{eq:guarantee} is therefore a monotone-risk
statement, to which Conformal Risk Control~\cite{crc2022} applies
directly, but with an expectation guarantee. It bounds the calibration-averaged
risk, not the probability that a given calibrated threshold exceeds
$\alpha$. We use Learn-then-Test~\cite{ltt2021} for the
high-probability form. For a finite grid $\lambda_1 > \lambda_2 >
\cdots > \lambda_J$ ordered from least to most conservative, we compute
the empirical risk $\hat R(\lambda_j) = \tfrac{1}{n}\sum_i L(X_i,\lambda_j)$
on $n$ calibration scenarios and a Hoeffding--Bentkus $p$-value for the
null hypothesis $H_j: \mathbb{E}[L(\cdot,\lambda_j)] > \alpha$, then apply
fixed-sequence testing starting from the most conservative $\lambda$.
Every $\lambda_j$ whose null is rejected satisfies
\eqref{eq:guarantee} simultaneously, with family-wise error controlled
at $\delta$, and $\hat\lambda$ is the least conservative such $\lambda_j$
(largest autonomy). The guarantee is therefore about the miss rate of the
alert, over scenarios exchangeable with the calibration scenarios, with
confidence $1-\delta$ over the calibration draw. We report the violation
frequency of both procedures over resplits rather than assuming it.

The marginal target is weaker than it may sound. With harm in a
fraction $\pi$ of scenarios, a marginal miss probability $\alpha$
leaves up to a fraction $\alpha/\pi$ of harmful scenarios unalerted, which is
$32\,\%$ at $\alpha=0.05$ and $\pi=0.158$ in our data ($24\,\%$ observed,
Section~\ref{sec:res-cost}).

\subsection{Trigger score}
\label{sec:trigger-score}
At each decision tick the geometric score $u_t$ measures how far a
predicted future position of any nearby agent intrudes into the ego
vehicle's own near-term extent. The ego's constant-heading,
constant-speed extrapolation over $T$ steps,
$\{p^{\mathrm{ego}}_\tau\}_{\tau=1}^{T}$, is the same route-agnostic motion
model used by the harm metric (Section~\ref{sec:harm}), deliberately not the
planned path. Each predicted offset of agent $i$, mode $k$, is split into a
longitudinal component $a^{(i,k)}_\tau$ and a lateral component
$\ell^{(i,k)}_\tau$ with margins
$m^{\mathrm{lon}}_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(L_{\mathrm{ego}}+L_i)+c_{\mathrm{lon}}$
and $m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(W_{\mathrm{ego}}+W_i)+c_{\mathrm{lat}}$.
The intrusion is
\begin{equation}
\rho^{(i,k)}_\tau = \max\!\left(0,\ 1 - \sqrt{
\left(\frac{a^{(i,k)}_\tau}{m^{\mathrm{lon}}_i}\right)^{\!2} +
\left(\frac{\ell^{(i,k)}_\tau}{m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i}\right)^{\!2}}\;
\right) m^{\mathrm{lat}}_i,
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
and $u_t = \max_{i,k,\tau} \rho^{(i,k)}_\tau$. The length-aware box is a
modeling choice made during development. Our ablation
(Supplementary Section~S14) finds no measurable benefit over a width-only
isotropic radius, so we report it as a design option.

\subsection{Fallback maneuver}
\label{sec:mrm}
On triggering, the vehicle executes the minimum-risk maneuver defined
by UN Regulation No.~157 (Automated Lane Keeping Systems)
\S\,5.5.1~\cite{unr157}: decelerate inside the current lane with a
deceleration demand not exceeding $4.0\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, lane-keeping
control otherwise unchanged, holding the vehicle stationary once
stopped. This is enforced by a proportional controller tracking the
target speed profile $v(t) = \max(v_0 - 4.0\,t,\,0)$ from the moment of
triggering.

\subsection{Weighted repair under covariate shift (a heuristic)}
\label{sec:shift}
The certificate in \eqref{eq:guarantee} assumes calibration and
deployment scenarios are exchangeable. For covariate shift we reweight
calibration scenarios by an estimated density ratio $w(x)$ from a domain
classifier on pre-deployment covariates (agent counts and speeds, initial
gap, route length) and unlabeled target scenarios, following weighted
conformal prediction~\cite{tibshirani2019}, and test the weighted risk with
the Hoeffding--Bentkus $p$-value at the Kish effective sample size
$n_{\mathrm{eff}} = (\sum_i w_i)^2 / \sum_i w_i^2$. Because the weights are
estimated and clipped and $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is an approximation, this is a
heuristic repair with no finite-sample guarantee. As the weights
concentrate, $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ shrinks and the test certifies a more
conservative threshold, and eventually none, which reports when the source
data cannot support a guarantee for the target.

\subsection{Evaluating every trigger by lookup}
\label{sec:exact-eval}
Fix a scenario $X$ and its random seed. The \emph{reference run}
$R(X)$ disables the fallback. For a decision tick $j$, the \emph{forced
run} $F(X,j)$ is identical to $R(X)$ until tick $j$ and then executes the
MRM for the rest of the episode. Let $\Phi(X,j)$ denote any outcome of
$F(X,j)$ (miss, stop, induced collision, residual harm, route completion)
and $\Phi(X,\infty)$ the outcome of $R(X)$. Fig.~\ref{fig:protocol}
summarizes the procedure.

\begin{observation}[Firing-tick sufficiency]
\label{prop:exact}
Assume (A1) the simulator, background traffic and predictor are
deterministic functions of the seed and of the ego's trajectory, so two
runs with the same seed and the same ego control up to a step have
identical states up to that step; (A2) the trigger's decision at tick $j$
depends only on the state history up to tick $j$; (A3) once the fallback
fires it is absorbing, and the control after firing depends only on the
state at firing. Then for every trigger with firing tick
$\tau(X)\in\{0,\Delta,2\Delta,\dots\}\cup\{\infty\}$, the run under the trigger is
$F(X,\tau(X))$, and every outcome equals $\Phi(X,\tau(X))$. This holds
for any score, threshold, online update rule and actuation latency
$\ell$ (for which the run is $F(X,\tau(X)+\ell)$).
\end{observation}
\begin{IEEEproof}
Until $\tau$ the control is that of $R(X)$ by (A3) and the states equal
those of $R(X)$ by (A1), so by (A2) the trigger's decisions, and hence
$\tau$, are determined by $R(X)$ alone. At $\tau$ the control depends
only on the state at $\tau$ (A3), as in $F(X,\tau)$, and both runs have
equal states afterwards by (A1).
\end{IEEEproof}
Two corollaries follow. Alert-level indicators need no forced run, since
$L$ and $S$ are functions of $R(X)$ and $\tau$ alone, and the consequences of
the fallback need only $n_{\text{ticks}}$ forced rollouts per scenario (16.8
on average, at most 26), after which any number of triggers is evaluated by
lookup, where direct execution needs over 7{,}000 rollouts per scenario here.
The observation is elementary and its value is practical: an exhaustive,
paired comparison becomes affordable. Assumption (A1) is an idealization,
so we measure its violation. Over the first 33{,}761 forced rollouts
(2{,}000 scenarios), 98.4\,\% agreed with the reference run to within 1\,cm
before firing, the pre-fire time-to-collision trace differed in 1.2\,\%, and
the pre-fire harm status in one rollout (0.003\,\%). We report every
analysis on all scenarios and after dropping the 3.6\,\% with any flagged
drift. We also executed four triggers directly on 252 random test scenarios
(1{,}008 scenario--trigger pairs, one with a one-tick latency). Firing step,
miss, unnecessary stop and induced collision agreed with the lookup in all
1{,}008 pairs (upper bound 0.4\,\%), and harm anywhere in the run in all but
one (Supplementary Section~S7). The lookup is exact only under (A1), but it
matched direct execution within this tolerance.

\subsection{Outcome-level certification and a counterfactual trigger}
\label{sec:outcome}
The forced rollouts also give, for every scenario, the outcome of firing at
each tick. Let the \emph{safe window} $W(X)$ be the set of firing ticks
(with ``never'') whose executed run has no harm, $F(X)$ its first tick,
and $O(X,\lambda)=\mathbb{1}[\text{harm in the run under }\lambda]$ the
\emph{outcome loss}, with a total-harm variant that also counts induced
collisions. $O$ is not monotone in $\lambda$ and $W(X)$ is empty when harm
is unavoidable. Learn-then-Test needs a monotone order only to be powerful,
not to be valid: any order fixed before looking at the calibration data
yields a valid family-wise guarantee. We therefore order the threshold
grid by the risk of $O$ estimated out of fold on a training split, run
fixed-sequence testing along that order on a separate calibration split,
and take among the certified thresholds the one with the fewest
unnecessary stops. A threshold chosen on one split also yields a
one-sided Clopper--Pearson upper bound on outcome harm from a fresh split.

The same table supervises a learned trigger. A gradient-boosted classifier
predicts, at each decision tick, whether the no-fallback run is harmful
and firing is not too early ($t \ge F(X)$), from the stored scores,
time-to-collision, headway distance, agent counts, predicted gaps and
scenario covariates, and its predicted probability is the trigger score.
For a delay of $\ell$ ticks the label is shifted to $t+\ell \ge F(X)$,
because a decision at $t$ acts at $t+\ell$. We call this
\emph{counterfactual safe-window certification} and report its hypotheses
as pre-registered (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Simulation environment}
All rollouts are executed headless in MetaDrive, replaying scenarios
converted by ScenarioNet~\cite{scenarionet2023} from Argoverse~2 Motion
Forecasting~\cite{av2motion2021} and nuScenes~\cite{nuscenes2020}.
Non-ego traffic is controlled reactively by the Intelligent Driver
Model~\cite{idm2000}, not replayed open-loop from the log. Pure log
replay roughly tripled the no-fallback collision rate on a 1{,}000-scenario
diagnostic sample, because logged agents cannot react to an ego vehicle
that deviates from its log, precisely what a fallback maneuver produces,
and would misrepresent the cost of stopping. The ego follows its logged route under IDM longitudinal control (MetaDrive's TrajectoryIDMPolicy) until the fallback engages, so the study concerns the trigger, not planning quality.

\subsection{Harm labeling}
\label{sec:harm}
A rollout is labeled harmful using the nuPlan closed-loop safety
definition~\cite{nuplan2024}: an at-fault collision, or a
time-to-collision below $0.95\,\mathrm{s}$ computed over a $3.0\,$s
horizon at $0.1\,$s resolution, not evaluated while the ego is
stationary or against tracks behind it. Fault attribution follows the
nuPlan convention. A collision the ego suffers while stationary, or while
struck from behind, is not at-fault and never counts as a missed
intervention. It is recorded as an \emph{induced collision}, the
measurable cost of a false or premature trigger.

\subsection{Predictor}
The trajectory predictor is AutoBot~\cite{autobot2022}, one of many
learned multi-modal forecasters~\cite{shi2025survey} (6 modes, 2.1\,s
history, 6\,s horizon), trained with the UniTraj framework~\cite{unitraj2024}
on the Argoverse~2 Motion Forecasting training split (minADE$_6$ = 1.09\,m
on held-out validation scenes). It runs online inside the closed loop, so
its input is the actual, possibly ego-deviated, simulator history, with
the UniTraj preprocessing unchanged. Vehicles are predicted by the
network. Pedestrians and cyclists, which the checkpoint was not trained
on, receive constant-velocity forecasts.

\subsection{Datasets and splits}
\label{sec:data}
All scenarios come from Argoverse~2 validation, disjoint from the
predictor's training data, split deterministically by scenario identifier
into development (14{,}990), calibration (4{,}971) and test (5{,}027) sets
(design choices used the development split only). The shifted targets are
nuScenes (9{,}041 scenarios of 8.1\,s, Boston and Singapore) and, under a
separate pre-registered amendment, 1{,}000 Waymo Open
Motion~\cite{waymo2021} validation scenarios (9.1\,s). Scenarios in which
the ego moves less than 10\,m are excluded, as in ScenarioNet. The
pre-registered evaluation uses 2{,}000 calibration, 2{,}000 test and
1{,}500 nuScenes scenarios, of which ten Argoverse~2 scenarios with
unmatched per-agent records are dropped from every analysis, leaving
1{,}994, 1{,}996 and 1{,}504. Decisions are taken at 2\,Hz, with identical
miss rates at 10\,Hz on a paired development comparison.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}
All methods use the same predictor, the same MRM and the same test
scenarios, and are evaluated by lookup (Section~\ref{sec:exact-eval}).
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{T0 --- time-to-collision threshold} (physics trigger, no learned component): fire when the nuPlan time-to-collision, projected at constant speed and heading, falls below a threshold; certified with LTT or tuned. Headway distance is a second physics variant.
\item \textbf{T1 --- confidence threshold} (uncertainty practice): fire when
$1-\max_k p_k$ of the network-predicted vehicle whose predicted modes come
closest to the ego exceeds a threshold. Agents the network does not
predict (pedestrians, cyclists, distant vehicles) are excluded from this
score. The threshold is tuned on calibration data to the most permissive
value with empirical miss rate $\le\alpha$. A variant that uses the
nearest agent ahead is also evaluated.
\item \textbf{T2 --- ensemble threshold}: the same tuning, applied to the
spread of endpoint predictions across five Monte Carlo dropout passes
\cite{galghahramani2016}.
\item \textbf{T3 --- open-loop conformal}: inflate every predicted mode by
the split-conformal radius of the predictor's 3\,s error on held-out
Argoverse~2 logs at level $\alpha$~\cite{vovk2005}, and fire when the
inflated region touches the ego. This is the guarantee-on-the-prediction
design that the paper argues is insufficient.
\item \textbf{Tuned geometric}: our score with an empirically tuned
threshold and no certificate. It isolates the contribution of the
calibrator.
\item \textbf{CDT}~\cite{cdt2024} and \textbf{ACI}~\cite{aci2021}: online
decision- and prediction-level adaptation, updated after every
scenario. Each uses the step size that performs best on test, which
favors the baselines. Choosing it on calibration data instead gives CDT
4.6\,\% missed and 25.1\,\% stops, and ACI the same result.
\item \textbf{Oracle}: the best threshold in hindsight on test data.
It is not deployable and is reported as an upper bound.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}
For every method we report the missed-intervention, unnecessary-stop and
fallback-induced-collision rates, autonomy (the fraction of scenarios
completed without the fallback) and route completion, with Wilson 95\,\%
intervals. Validity of each certificate is assessed over 200 random
half-and-half resplits, without replacement, of the pooled 3{,}990
calibration and test scenarios. A valid certificate keeps the frequency
of test miss rate above $\alpha$ at or below $\delta$. The resplits
overlap, so they are not independent draws: the frequency estimates the
violation probability for this pool, with Monte Carlo error (Wilson
95\,\% interval [6.2, 14.4] for 9.5\,\%, [36.8, 50.4] for 43.5\,\%).
Scenarios from one place may be dependent. Resplitting whole spatial
groups (ego starts within 150\,m, a proxy) raises LTT's violation
frequency to 17.0\,\% [12.4, 22.8], above $\delta$, although group
bootstrap intervals for the miss rates barely widen (Supplementary
Section~S12). Harm uses the nuPlan definition above, and
Section~\ref{sec:res-robust} repeats the key comparison under three
alternatives.

\subsection{Pre-registration and reproducibility}
\label{sec:prereg}
The harm definition, $\alpha = 0.05$, $\delta = 0.10$, and the refutation
criteria for three hypotheses were recorded in a version-controlled note
before any calibration or test data were collected: \textbf{H1}, that
open-loop metrics rank triggers differently from closed-loop outcomes;
\textbf{H2}, that a certified trigger achieves a better
safety--conservatism trade-off than a tuned fixed threshold; and
\textbf{H3}, that a threshold certified on Argoverse~2 loses its
guarantee on nuScenes. A later amendment, written before any Waymo
outcome existed, added \textbf{H3-W}, the same hypothesis on Waymo, and
a few-label recalibration study. The note carries further dated
amendments (a corrected confidence baseline, the exactness measurements,
robustness checks, a direct-execution check, and a strict-review
amendment that added the TTC baseline, noise-corrected validity,
outcome-level certification and the learned counterfactual trigger), each
written before the corresponding outcomes were evaluated, and it labels
the analyses run afterwards as post hoc. That last amendment was written
after the main results were known, and six of its ten pre-registered
claims were refuted (Supplementary Section~S17).

\emph{Data and code availability.} Code, the pre-registration note with
all amendments, configuration files and per-scenario rollout records are
available at \url{https://github.com/hwaqas160/paper03-risk-fallback}
and archived at \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23054382}. Each
scenario's record contains every score trace and outcome, so every table
can be regenerated without re-simulation.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
All results use $\alpha = 0.05$ and $\delta = 0.10$, 1{,}994 calibration
and 1{,}996 test scenarios from Argoverse~2, and 1{,}504 nuScenes
scenarios, with Wilson 95\,\% intervals unless stated. Dropping the 3.6\,\% of scenarios with any flagged simulator drift changes no conclusion below.
Sections~\ref{sec:res-main}--\ref{sec:ablation} and the shift study are pre-registered,
as is the Waymo recalibration (R1--R4). Section~\ref{sec:res-outcome} and the TTC
baseline come from a later amendment written after the main results were
known, and the consequences of the intervention, the class-conditional
comparison and the robustness checks are post hoc. Each is labeled.

\subsection{Does a certificate keep its promise, and what does it cost? (RQ1)}
\label{sec:res-main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives every method at its operating point on the
Argoverse~2 test set. The certified geometric trigger (LTT) fails to
pre-empt harm in 3.7\,\% of all scenarios [95\,\% CI 2.9--4.6], below the
5\,\% target, which is 24\,\% of the scenarios that are harmful, with
28.5\,\% unnecessary stops and 2.6\,\% fallback-induced collisions. The last
column repeats the calibration on 200 random resplits. A valid certificate
keeps the uncorrected frequency at or below $\delta = 10\,\%$, and LTT does
(9.5\,\%). That count also includes test-sample noise, so we add a
corrected count that only counts resplits whose test miss is clearly above
$\alpha$. Empirical tuning, Conformal Risk Control (CRC), which guarantees
only an expectation, and the tuned confidence trigger are clearly violated
under both counts (43.5\,\%, 41.0\,\% and 50.5\,\% uncorrected; 12--15\,\%
corrected), while LTT is not violated under either. Among the predictor-based
triggers, certification is therefore the only design that reliably keeps its
target at a useful operating point (the uncertified TTC threshold sits far
below the target and is only indeterminate). Fig.~\ref{fig:operating-points} plots every method against
the geometric score's own curve as its threshold varies, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:validity} shows the resplit distributions behind the
violation column. CRC and empirical tuning select the same threshold here, because no grid point falls between the calibration risks 4.81\,\% and 5.02\,\%.

\emph{The certificate has a price, and it does not win the trade-off.}
The tuned geometric threshold and CDT both stop less often than LTT
(paired bootstrap difference $-2.5$ points, 95\,\% CI $[-3.2,-1.8]$ against
tuned). Hypothesis H2 required no baseline to be within 3 points of the
certified trigger. It is \textbf{refuted}. The supportable claim is
narrower: among the designs that keep their promise at a useful operating
point, the certified one is the only one that reliably meets it. Open-loop
conformal never violates, but fires in 73.8\,\% of scenarios.

\emph{A physics trigger dominates at zero latency.} A time-to-collision
threshold, certified with the same procedure, meets the target with
3.1\,\% missed and only 7.1\,\% unnecessary stops and 1.0\,\% induced
collisions (Table~\ref{tab:main}), against 28.5\,\% for the
predictor-based geometric trigger. The comparison is not neutral, because
the harm label is itself defined by time-to-collision, and it holds in
three of four alternative harm definitions, including collision only
(Supplementary Section~S14). Under this label the predictor adds nothing
at zero latency, and the confidence and ensemble triggers, which stop
40--45 points more often than the geometric trigger, are weak
comparators, in part because this predictor's mode probabilities are
weakly informative (AUROC 0.63) and miscalibrated (ECE 0.25).
ACI (1.3\,\% at 45\,\%) and open-loop conformal (0.0\,\% at 73.8\,\%) show a guarantee on the prediction not translating into a useful decision.

\subsection{Do open-loop metrics choose the closed-loop trigger? (RQ2)}
\label{sec:res-h1}
Ranking six trigger scores by open-loop AUROC of their scenario peak and
by closed-loop unnecessary stops at each certified threshold
(Supplementary Section~S14), the rankings agree at the top and diverge
below it: Kendall's $\tau = 0.60$ on Argoverse~2, $0.47$ on nuScenes and
$0.73$ on Waymo. The pre-registered rule refutes H1 only if $\tau \ge 0.8$
and the top score coincides in every setting, so H1 is not refuted, but
the evidence is weak. The clearest disagreement is at the bottom: the
confidence score has AUROC $0.45$, below chance, yet a threshold on it
still reaches the miss target at 75.8\,\% stops, by firing
indiscriminately.

\subsection{Ablations and latency (RQ4)}
\label{sec:ablation}
Ablating the geometric score (Supplementary Section~S14), a route-following
corridor makes the trigger fire in essentially every scenario (84.9\,\%
stops), consistent with the route reaching conflict points that the
kinematic harm label does not count, replacing geometry with confidence
or ensemble spread costs 44--47 points of stops, and a width-only
isotropic radius performs the same as the length-aware box (27.5\,\% versus
28.5\,\%). One decision tick (0.5\,s) of delay lifts the alert-level miss rate
above the target (5.3\,\%) and two ticks give 9.1\,\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:shift-latency}b),
and the zero-latency certificate is clearly violated at both (53\,\% and
100\,\% corrected). Calibrating the alert under the deployed latency gives
3.6--4.1\,\% missed at 34.4\,\% and 42.5\,\% stops and is not violated under
the corrected count (1.5\,\% and 0.5\,\%; its uncorrected counts, 12.0\,\% and
11.5\,\%, are ambiguous). The TTC trigger is much more latency-sensitive: its
zero-latency certificate misses 11.7\,\% and 14.3\,\%, and calibrating under
latency needs 84.9\,\% stops, essentially always firing, against 34.4\,\% and
42.5\,\% for the geometric score.

\subsection{Outcome-level certification, with and without latency}
\label{sec:res-outcome}
The alert certificate leaves 6.5\,\% harm after the fallback. We therefore
certify the residual harm of the executed run at 7.5\,\% (no fallback leaves
15.1\,\%, always firing 4.1\,\%, so 5\,\% is barely reachable) with
risk-ordered fixed-sequence testing (Section~\ref{sec:outcome}), calibrating
on 904 fresh scenarios and testing on 2{,}900 others
(Table~\ref{tab:outcome}). At zero latency every score certifies and meets
the target. The TTC trigger is cheapest (10.2\,\% unnecessary stops), the
learned counterfactual trigger is 3.3 points more expensive (paired CI
[2.2, 4.4], so the pre-registered claim that it beats TTC is
\textbf{refuted}), and the geometric score needs 32.8\,\%. At 0.5\,s of
latency the learned trigger, trained with delay-shifted labels, needs
25.5\,\% against 37.5\,\% for the geometric score (paired CI
$[-14.0,-10.0]$) and 84.9\,\% for TTC, and at 1.0\,s it needs 34.2\,\% while
the other two need 84.9\,\%. The pre-registered latency claim therefore
\textbf{holds}: no trigger dominates, TTC is cheapest without delay and the
learned trigger once the delay is 0.5\,s or more.

A feature ablation shows what drives the gain. A learned trigger with
physics features only stops 24.8\,\% at 0.5\,s, as many as the full one
(25.5\,\%; paired CI of the difference $[-0.1, 1.6]$), and one with
predictor features only stops 21.5\,\%, so the pre-registered claim that the
predictor adds lead time beyond physics is \textbf{refuted}: the gain over a
plain TTC threshold comes from the counterfactual, delay-shifted labels.
With a second AutoBot and with Wayformer (452 training and 452 calibration
scenarios) the learned trigger stops 49\,\% and 43\,\% at 0.5\,s against
85\,\% for the others (claim holds), although at zero latency it is worse than
the geometric score on the second AutoBot. All three scores meet the target
on 6 of 8 shift targets (Supplementary Section~S18). A fresh-split audit bounds the alert-level threshold's outcome harm at 7.9\,\% and covers realized harm except on nuScenes and, narrowly, Palo Alto (Section~S16).

The Argoverse~2 certificate does not transfer. On nuScenes the threshold
certified at a 5\,\% target misses 13.2\,\% [11.6, 15.0], 2.6 times the
target and with an interval that excludes it. The pre-registered
hypothesis H3 is \textbf{supported} (15.2\,\% after dropping drift
scenarios). Part of this is a floor rather than shift: 5.0\,\% of
nuScenes scenarios already contain harm at the first decision tick, which
even a trigger that always fires cannot prevent (0.1\,\% on
Argoverse~2). That floor equals the target, so no threshold can be
certified at $\alpha = 0.05$ on nuScenes. With those scenarios excluded
(post hoc), the shifted miss rate is 9.2\,\% [7.8, 10.8], still above the
target. These intervals treat scenarios as independent, but the 1{,}504 nuScenes scenarios come from only 37 scenes. Resampling scenes widens the interval to [6.1, 21.0], which still excludes the target, while a second, disjoint set of 23 scenes gives 9.5\,\% with scene-level interval [3.9, 16.5], which does not: the direction of the failure replicates but its size is uncertain (Supplementary Section~S6). A domain classifier on the covariates separates Argoverse~2 from nuScenes (cross-validated AUROC 0.97) far more easily than from Waymo (0.91), so the certificate failed under the larger measured shift and held under the smaller. Six further natural shifts, the Argoverse~2 cities held out one at a time, add evidence: the certificate met the target in four of six (67\,\%, below the 80\,\% we pre-registered, so that claim is refuted) and failed in Miami (6.1\,\% [4.8, 7.8]) and Washington, DC (5.3\,\%), the two cities with the highest harm rates. Across these and the two datasets the held-out miss rate tracks the target's harm rate (Spearman 0.90, $n=8$, post hoc), but estimating that rate from unlabeled alerts to correct the target was unreliable and did not help (Supplementary Sections~S10 and S15).

Density-ratio weighting, using only unlabeled target covariates, moves
the miss rate from 13.2\,\% to 7.0\,\%, still above the target, and
doubles unnecessary stops (26.1\,\% to 52.4\,\%). On the avoidable subset
it reaches 2.2\,\%, below the target, but its effective sample size is only
68, so the certificate is achieved almost entirely through conservatism
(54.5\,\% stops).

\subsection{What the intervention does, and a class-conditional certificate (post hoc)}
\label{sec:res-cost}
The certified quantity is the alert. At the certified threshold on
Argoverse~2, 3.7\,\% of scenarios are missed, in a further 1.7\,\% the
fallback fires before harm but harm still occurs, in 9.8\,\% harm is
prevented, in 1.1\,\% of the harmless scenarios the intervened run contains
harm, and 2.6\,\% suffer an induced collision
(Supplementary Section~S19). Harm in the intervened run is therefore
6.5\,\% (15.1\,\% without a fallback), almost twice the certified miss
rate; on the fresh split it is 5.0\,\% [3.7, 6.6] against 3.4\,\% missed. A
trigger that always fires creates harm in 3.1\,\% of scenarios, and on
nuScenes the same threshold leaves 15.6\,\%. Induced collisions are other
vehicles striking the ego, at low to moderate speed (median speed
difference 2.4\,m/s), typically 6.5\,s after firing and often where it
starts at standstill, so this cost may partly reflect simulated traffic that
does not yield to a stationary vehicle (Supplementary Section~S6).

\emph{Class-conditional certification and reverse shift.} A Luo-style class-conditional certificate at $\epsilon=0.05$ is equivalent to always firing, and at $\epsilon=0.32$ its conditional miss on nuScenes is 66\,\% against a 32\,\% target (Supplementary Section~S1). Certifying on nuScenes and deploying on Argoverse~2 is safe but conservative (Section~S2).

\subsection{Second shifted dataset and recalibration}
\label{sec:res-waymo}
\emph{Waymo Open Motion.} Table~\ref{tab:shift} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:shift-latency}a deploy the same
Argoverse~2-certified threshold, unchanged, on 1{,}000 Waymo Open Motion
validation scenarios~\cite{waymo2021} (9.1\,s, 1\,s of history). It misses 2.7\,\% [1.9, 3.9], below the
5\,\% target. Hypothesis H3-W was pre-registered with the expectation
that this interval would exclude $\alpha$ from above, as on nuScenes; it
does exclude $\alpha$, but from below, so H3-W is \textbf{refuted} and
failure under shift is dataset-dependent. A density-ratio-weighted repair, applied for
consistency even though nothing here needed repairing, lowers the miss
rate further to 1.2\,\% [0.7, 2.1] at a small increase in stops (45.1\,\%
to 50.2\,\%).

\emph{Waymo recalibration (pre-registered R1--R4).} Drawing $k$ labeled
Waymo scenes at random (200 draws; Supplementary Section~S3), uncertified
tuning violates the target in 69.5\,\% and 59\,\% of draws with 40 and 60
target scenes (\textbf{R1 holds}). Weighting the Argoverse~2 calibration
set with 60 Waymo scenes ($n_{\mathrm{eff}} = 232$) keeps validity (0\,\%
violation, \textbf{R2 holds}) at a cost of 21.3 points of unnecessary
stops (\textbf{R3 holds}). \textbf{R4}, which restates H3-W, is refuted:
the source certificate already holds on Waymo. Few labeled target scenes
are dangerous without a certificate even when the source certificate
transfers.

\emph{Sensitivity.} Non-reactive replay traffic, a 2.0\,m/s$^2$ comfort
deceleration and 10\,Hz decisions leave the certified miss rate below the
target (Supplementary Section~S4). Thresholds certified on reactive traffic
and deployed on replay traffic miss 3.7\,\% [2.2, 6.1] with 35.4\,\% stops
against 71.7\,\% for the tuned confidence trigger (Section~S11).

\subsection{Robustness checks (post hoc)}
\label{sec:res-robust}
Two objections are addressed on data not used above, in checks
pre-registered as post hoc before running.

\emph{Does the geometric score's advantage depend on matching the harm
label?} The score and the nuPlan label are both proximity- and time-to-collision-based. Re-certifying LTT and the best of T1/T2 under three alternative harm definitions (collision only with $\alpha = 0.01$, and time-to-collision thresholds of 0.5\,s and 1.5\,s, Supplementary Section~S14), the geometric score's advantage over the confidence and ensemble scores survives every definition by at least 20 points of stops. The TTC trigger, however, beats the geometric score under three of the four, so the advantage is over uncertainty-based triggers only.

\emph{Does validity replicate on unseen data and a second predictor?} We recalibrate LTT on 904 fresh Argoverse~2 scenarios, test on a further 904, and repeat with an independently trained predictor (minADE$_6 = 0.854$ against 1.09; T3 and ACI need open-loop error files and are omitted, T2 is added by re-running reference runs with dropout). LTT's validity replicates in both settings (7.5\,\% and 9.0\,\% violations against 46--51\,\% for the uncertified baselines) and its miss rate stays below $\alpha$ (Supplementary Section~S8).

%=======================================================================
\section{Scope and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{What we claim, and what we do not.} We claim that certification makes a closed-loop trigger keep its stated miss rate, that the outcome of the executed fallback can be certified with risk-ordered testing, that no trigger dominates across latency, and that the guarantee is fragile to shift and dependence. We do not claim a better trigger at zero latency (a TTC threshold is cheaper), a guarantee under shift, or one on outcomes other than those calibrated.

\emph{The harm label and the trigger.} Harm is defined by time-to-collision, so a TTC trigger is close to the label and its zero-latency advantage is partly structural. It disappears under latency because a threshold on current TTC fires only just before harm, and a learned trigger with delay-shifted counterfactual labels recovers it from physics or predictor features alike (Section~\ref{sec:res-outcome}). Collision-only harm weakens but does not remove the TTC advantage. The supervised vehicle is a rule-based driver, not a learned planner, so results concern triggering.

\emph{Alert versus outcome.} The alert certificate leaves 6.5\,\% harm after the fallback and 24\,\% of harmful scenarios unalerted (58\,\% on nuScenes). The outcome certificate of Section~\ref{sec:outcome} bounds the executed run's harm, but only as well as the calibration data resemble deployment. The 5.0\,\% first-tick floor on nuScenes shows that a miss metric on harm anywhere in a replayed snippet mixes preventable events with events already under way, and we propose an avoidable-only variant for future benchmarks.

\emph{Simulator, predictor, perception and dependence.} All results use MetaDrive with rule-based (IDM) agents, so whether the ordering of triggers survives learned agent behavior is open, and the induced-collision rate is best read as an upper bound. The geometric trigger's validity and its gap to the uncertainty triggers replicate with a second AutoBot and with Wayformer (Supplementary Section~S13). The predictor receives agent states, which isolates triggering from perception error. All guarantees treat scenarios as exchangeable, which nearby scenarios may violate: our nuScenes samples span only 37 and 23 scenes, and spatial-group resplits leave the high-probability statement indeterminate.

%=======================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
A certificate on a fallback trigger bounds what it was calibrated to bound, and what that is matters. With the fallback executed, a Learn-then-Test certified alert keeps its 5\,\% target in distribution (3.7\,\% missed, violations in 9.5\,\% of resplits against 43.5\,\% uncertified) but leaves 6.5\,\% harm after the fallback. Risk-ordered testing certifies the executed outcome directly (5.4\,\% realized against a 7.5\,\% target). Which trigger is cheapest depends on latency: a certified time-to-collision trigger needs 10\,\% unnecessary stops without delay and 85\,\% at 0.5\,s, where a learned counterfactual trigger needs 26\,\%; confidence and ensemble triggers need 68--71\,\% at the alert level. The certificate fails on nuScenes and in two of six held-out cities, both with high harm rates, but holds on Waymo. For the setting studied, these findings argue for certifying the outcome at the deployed latency, auditing it on data that resemble deployment, and checking a physics baseline first; the lookup protocol makes each inexpensive to repeat.
